Imagine a short story published in the magazine of a college in Uttar Pradesh. A student named Kavita wins a prize for obedience after years of being praised for remaining silent. On the day of the ceremony, she leaves the certificate on a desk and walks out. The story does not explain why she leaves, and the writer supplies no note about its meaning.

Some students think the ending celebrates freedom, while others see a refusal of responsibility. The principal describes the story as a lesson in discipline, but another reader recognises the language of old moral tales. Kavita's wish to be recognised as a good student also seems to conflict with her final action. How did these different meanings arise, and who has the authority to decide which one is correct?

**Post-structuralism** is a broad approach that questions the stability of meaning, knowledge and human identity. This article in the series on literary theory and cultural studies follows four thinkers who approached those questions differently. Roland Barthes examines authorship, Michel Foucault studies the historical organisation of knowledge, and Julia Kristeva studies the meeting of texts. Jacques Lacan helps explain why even the person who speaks cannot fully control meaning.

## Where post-structuralism came from

### From the study of language to the limits of structure

At the beginning of the twentieth century, Ferdinand de Saussure offered a new way to understand language. His *Course in General Linguistics* appeared in 1916, after his death. A linguistic sign joins a **signifier**, such as a sound pattern, to a **signified**, the concept it expresses. The relationship is arbitrary in the sense that no natural bond fixes the sound to the concept.

Saussure also argued that linguistic units gain their value through their differences from other units. A word makes sense within a system of relationships, rather than standing alone. A traffic light offers a simple comparison because its red signal gains meaning within an organised system. The signal does not contain the idea of stopping as a physical property of red light.

Structuralism extended this kind of reasoning into the study of stories, myths and cultural practices. In a story about a hero, a critic might examine the contrast between courage and cowardice. Individual adventures could then be compared with other stories that use the same pattern. The critic seeks the underlying system that makes particular stories intelligible.

Post-structuralist thought inherits much of this attention to relations, signs and cultural conventions. However, it questions the possibility of discovering a complete system that guarantees the final meaning of everything within it. Kavita's certificate illustrates the problem because obedience may imply goodness in one account and submission in another. The relationship between obedience and goodness can itself become an object of criticism.

### A change in the intellectual climate

In France during the 1960s, literary theory developed alongside debates in philosophy, linguistics and psychoanalysis. Structuralist models were influential, but their assumptions were being questioned even by thinkers who had worked with them. Post-structuralism emerged from these debates rather than from a single manifesto or a formally organised school. The name covers related projects whose methods and conclusions can differ considerably.

The political unrest of May 1968 in France placed authority under especially visible pressure. Student protests and a vast strike challenged established institutions during that period. These events help explain the wider intellectual interest in authority, although they did not create all the theories discussed here. Lacan's work on the ego and language, for instance, began much earlier.

The familiar idea that a person freely expresses a complete inner self was another subject of debate. If someone writes in a language learned from other people, that language carries inherited distinctions. Institutions also shape the categories through which a person understands a situation. A writer can use these resources creatively, yet cannot simply invent every condition of communication.

The word **subject** in this discussion refers to a person as a thinking, speaking and socially situated being. Post-structuralist approaches ask how such a subject comes to recognise and describe itself. They question the assumption that an independent self exists as the unquestioned source of every meaning. This does not imply that people are unreal or that human actions have no consequences.

### Did you know?

The National Library of France records two publication dates for Barthes's essay "The Death of the Author". Its first publication was in English in the American magazine *Aspen* in 1967. The French version appeared in *Mantéia* in 1968, which explains the two dates found in literary histories.

### Important points to remember

- Saussure's *Course in General Linguistics* appeared in 1916.
- Structuralism studies the relations underlying individual signs and texts.
- Post-structuralism questions the completeness and stability of such systems.
- It emerged through several related intellectual debates, not a single manifesto.
- French political unrest in May 1968 challenged established institutions.
- The subject becomes an object of study rather than an unquestioned source of meaning.
- Barthes's essay appeared in English in 1967 and French in 1968.

## The core ideas of post-structuralism

### Meaning and the limits of a final reading

In "The Death of the Author", Barthes asks readers to reconsider the authority granted to the writer. An explanation of a poem often begins with an account of its writer's feelings or private circumstances. Such information may be useful, but Barthes objects when it becomes the final answer to every interpretive question. His approach directs attention to the language and cultural resources that operate within writing.

Suppose Kavita's story was written by a student who intended to praise good behaviour. A passage could nevertheless bring the language of obedience into conflict with its own ending. Readers could notice this contrast without knowing the writer's personal life or receiving permission from the writer. Barthes's challenge is to the author's supposed power to close interpretation, not to the fact of authorship.

**Plurality** means that a text can sustain more than one line of meaning. The certificate might signify achievement, conformity or the pressure to please others. These readings gain strength from different connections within the same story. The fact that meanings can multiply does not make all unsupported guesses equally useful.

### Discourse and the production of knowledge

Foucault moves the discussion from a literary text towards the wider social conditions in which statements become meaningful. A **discourse** is a historically organised practice through which objects of knowledge and ways of discussing them are formed. It involves more than conversation or a collection of words. Rules about evidence, authorised speakers and accepted categories help determine what can count as knowledge.

Consider how the college identifies a good student through attendance records, marks and reports about conduct. Each record describes something, but their combination also creates an official picture of the student. That picture may shape future opportunities, even when it does not capture the whole person. Foucault asks how arrangements of this kind arise and acquire authority.

In *The Order of Things* (1966), Foucault investigates historical conditions that make particular fields of knowledge possible. He uses the term **episteme** for historically specific relations among discursive practices that organise knowledge. An episteme is not simply the opinion shared by everyone living at a particular time. It concerns conditions under which different forms of knowledge develop and operate.

His **archaeology** examines the rules and regularities that make statements possible within particular historical periods. His **genealogy**, influenced by Nietzsche, investigates historical struggles and practices through which present arrangements came into being. Neither method needs a story in which knowledge naturally progresses towards its present form. The apparently ordinary arrangements of the present may have developed through quite different historical paths.

### Power, knowledge and the making of subjects

In *Discipline and Punish* (1975), Foucault studies the emergence of modern disciplinary practices. **Disciplinary power** shapes conduct through observation, training, comparison and correction. It operates in institutions such as schools, prisons and military organisations. It does not function exclusively through commands from a king or government.

The connection called **power-knowledge** concerns how practices of power and the production of knowledge support one another. An examination gives an institution information about students and makes their performance comparable. Those results can then justify new forms of supervision and correction. The practices that generate knowledge also help organise the conduct of the people being studied.

A student who knows that attendance is recorded may arrive early without being directly ordered each morning. The possibility of observation becomes part of the student's own conduct. Foucault calls attention to this productive aspect of power because it forms habits and categories of persons. He does not suggest that every institutional practice has identical purposes or effects.

### Other texts within a text

Kristeva takes the discussion towards the relationship between writing and the language that precedes it. Drawing on Mikhail Bakhtin, she develops the concept of **intertextuality**, the transformation and interaction of texts within another text. A work participates in existing languages, literary forms and cultural statements. Its words cannot be understood as the completely private property of an isolated writer.

The prize story may draw on school regulations, moral tales and accounts of rebellious heroines. Even a reader who has never seen an earlier version can recognise traces of these familiar forms. The ending may change the function of those inherited materials by bringing them into an unexpected relationship. Intertextuality is therefore more extensive than a writer's deliberate quotation of another book.

### A subject divided by language

Lacan approaches language through his rereading of Freud's psychoanalysis. His theory of the unconscious stresses the relations among signifiers rather than treating the mind as fully transparent to itself. A speaker can intend one thing while the words used suggest connections beyond that intention. A slip of the tongue offers a familiar illustration, although no single slip proves a psychoanalytic interpretation.

For Lacan, the human subject takes shape through relations with images, language and other people. A student can identify with the ideal image of being a successful child while feeling unable to satisfy every demand. The **split subject** names this lack of complete unity in the speaking self. The person cannot simply step outside language and recover a perfectly independent identity.

### Important points to remember

- Barthes challenges the author's status as final authority over a text.
- Plurality refers to the coexistence of several lines of meaning.
- Foucault studies the historical rules that organise discourse.
- The episteme concerns historical conditions of knowledge.
- Archaeology studies regularities of discourse; genealogy investigates their historical formation through practices and struggles.
- Power and knowledge participate in the formation of subjects.
- Kristeva's intertextuality draws on Bakhtin.
- Lacan questions the full self-knowledge of the speaking subject.

## Thinkers and texts

### Roland Barthes (1915-1980): from the author to the text

Barthes had already used semiology to examine cultural meanings in *Mythologies* (1957). His later work increasingly investigates the multiplicity of writing instead of treating interpretation as the recovery of one organising code. "The Death of the Author" begins with a question about a description in Balzac's *Sarrasine*. Barthes asks whose voice speaks through the description, since it also carries conventional ideas about femininity.

He uses the word **scriptor** to displace the traditional image of the author as a sovereign origin. The act of writing brings inherited words into new relations rather than simply transmitting a fully formed inner message. The reader becomes the point at which different strands of writing meet. The death in the essay's title is therefore figurative.

In *S/Z* (1970), Barthes studies Balzac's novella through five interacting codes rather than reducing it to one explanation. He distinguishes the **readerly text**, which positions the reader largely as a consumer, from the **writerly text**, which invites active production of meaning. His distinction does not refer simply to whether the work is easy or difficult. It concerns the relation a reader can have to the text's activity.

In "From Work to Text" (1971), Barthes distinguishes a work as an object from the text as an activity of signification. A printed novel can be placed on a shelf, but the relations involved in reading cannot be stored in the same way. In *The Pleasure of the Text* (1973), he explores reading through pleasure and **jouissance**, often translated as bliss. The latter can unsettle familiar ways of reading and the reader's established sense of self.

### Michel Foucault (1926-1984): from the author to the institution

Foucault's "What Is an Author?" (1969) responds to the problem of authorship by asking how the author operates within discourse. He calls this role the **author-function**, a historically variable way of attributing, classifying and regulating certain texts. A writer's name can connect several books and influence the way they circulate. An anonymous notice and a signed literary work may consequently receive very different treatment.

For example, imagine that the college magazine attributes Kavita's story to the principal instead of a student. The words would remain the same, yet readers might approach the text differently. Foucault's question is not simply what the principal really meant. He asks what the attribution does within the institution and how that practice gained its authority.

In *The Archaeology of Knowledge* (1969), he explains his method for investigating discursive formations. In *Discipline and Punish*, he studies practices including the examination, hierarchical observation and normalising judgement. His discussion of Jeremy Bentham's **Panopticon**, a design for observation from a central position, illustrates a principle of disciplinary visibility. The possibility of being observed can shape behaviour without continuous actual observation.

*The History of Sexuality*, volume one, appeared in French in 1976. Foucault challenges the **repressive hypothesis**, according to which modern society's relation to sexuality is explained chiefly by silencing it. He examines how modern institutions have also encouraged speech about sexuality and organised knowledge around it. This study illustrates why the production of discourse matters alongside prohibition.

### Julia Kristeva (born 1941): the text in motion

Born in Bulgaria, Kristeva moved into the intellectual world of France and engaged with structuralism, psychoanalysis and Bakhtin's work. Her essay on Bakhtin, "Word, Dialogue and Novel", appeared in French in 1967. Its argument develops intertextuality as a way to rethink the apparently independent literary text. Her collection *Séméiotikè* appeared in 1969, and English readers encountered related essays in *Desire in Language* (1980).

In *Revolution in Poetic Language* (1974 in French), Kristeva distinguishes two interacting aspects of signification. The **semiotic** concerns bodily drives, rhythm and sound as forces within language. The **symbolic** concerns the organisation of meaning through grammar, distinctions and social rules. Both are involved in signifying activity, although different kinds of writing may bring their tension into greater prominence.

A lullaby provides a plain example of how rhythm, repetition and tone can matter beyond the dictionary meanings of its words. A poem can similarly disturb ordinary grammar through rhythm or unexpected associations. Kristeva's semiotic is not identical to semiotics, the general study of signs. Nor does she treat the symbolic as something that poetry can simply leave behind.

Her account of the subject in process emphasises the unstable formation of identity through these forces. Her later *Powers of Horror* appeared in French in 1980 and examines **abjection**, the disturbance of boundaries that sustain the self. Something rejected as outside the self can remain troubling because those boundaries are never entirely secure. Her work on these ideas also became important to later feminist criticism.

### Jacques Lacan (1901-1981): the subject and the Other

Lacan was a French psychoanalyst who reworked Freud with resources from philosophy and linguistics. His influential published account of the **mirror stage** dates from 1949, and his French collection *Écrits* appeared in 1966. In the mirror stage, the child identifies with an image of bodily unity that exceeds its present mastery of its movements. This identification helps form the ego, yet it also involves misrecognition because the image appears more complete than lived experience.

Lacan distinguishes the **Imaginary**, the **Symbolic** and the **Real** as registers of experience. The Imaginary involves images, identifications and relations of apparent unity. The Symbolic involves language, law and the network of social relations into which the subject enters. The Real is what resists full symbolisation, not simply the ordinary physical world.

The registers are interrelated rather than three stages that a person completes in a fixed sequence. Lacan also distinguishes the **big Other**, the place of language and symbolic authority, from particular people encountered in everyday life. A student's wish for recognition can involve the imagined standards of family, school and society. The student may continue to seek approval even after receiving a particular prize.

For Lacan, desire is linked to lack and cannot be reduced to a bodily need. Food can satisfy hunger, but the desire for recognition cannot be satisfied in precisely the same manner. A reader using Lacan might examine how a literary character repeatedly seeks approval through substitute objects or achievements. Such a reading is a theoretical interpretation, not a clinical diagnosis of a fictional person.

### An interesting story

In October 1966, Johns Hopkins University in Baltimore hosted a four-day symposium called "The Languages of Criticism and the Sciences of Man". Richard Macksey and Eugenio Donato helped organise the event, which brought European thinkers into discussion with American academics. Barthes and Lacan took part, as did Jacques Derrida. Derrida presented his influential criticism of the idea of a stable structure. The proceedings first appeared in 1970, and the university has since made historical recordings and transcriptions available. The gathering is a useful reminder that the turn towards post-structuralism developed through debate within structuralist circles. It was not a sudden declaration by a group whose members all shared a single theory.

## Reading with post-structuralism

### Back to the college magazine

The story of Kavita can now be read through four questions rather than one search for the writer's intention. Barthes draws attention to the meanings produced when the language of reward meets the refusal of the reward. Foucault asks how the school makes obedience an observable quality and gives certain people the authority to judge it. Kristeva asks how familiar moral stories and official language are transformed when they meet within this story.

Lacan adds a question about the subject who is supposed to be rewarded. Kavita might have learned to recognise herself through the image of a good student. Her decision to leave the certificate unsettles that image without necessarily revealing a completely independent self. Her act can have several meanings, and the text gives no direct access to her unconscious.

The four questions overlap, but their claims require evidence. A comparison of phrases may support a reading inspired by Barthes or Kristeva. An institutional record might support a historical reading inspired by Foucault. Repeated patterns of desire and identification within the story might support a Lacanian reading.

### Reading Shakespeare's Hamlet

In Shakespeare's *Hamlet*, the prince encounters demands from the Ghost, the court and his own reflections. A reading influenced by Barthes need not explain every tension in the play through Shakespeare's private beliefs. It can follow the competing meanings of duty, action and knowledge within the play. Foucault's method suggests further questions about who is permitted to speak and who is treated as credible.

Kristeva's approach invites attention to the way the play transforms inherited revenge tragedy conventions and religious language. The play within the play makes the act of representing an earlier event part of the drama itself. A Lacanian reading might examine Hamlet's relation to the demand of the Other and the problem of desire. This is a way to study the drama's patterns, not to settle the prince's medical or psychological condition.

### Important points to remember

- The same narrative can be examined through different theoretical questions.
- Barthes draws attention to multiple connections within writing.
- Foucault directs attention to institutions and historically authorised knowledge.
- Kristeva studies the transformation of earlier discourses and literary forms.
- Lacanian interpretation considers desire, identification and symbolic authority.
- Literary interpretations should identify the evidence on which they rest.

## Criticisms and legacy

### The case against post-structuralism

In *The Philosophical Discourse of Modernity* (1985 in German), Jürgen Habermas examines the problems he sees in several critiques of modern reason. His discussion of Foucault questions the grounds on which a critique of modern power can justify its own judgements. Nancy Fraser raises a related difficulty in her essay "Foucault on Modern Power: Empirical Insights and Normative Confusions" (1981). She distinguishes valuable historical insights about power from problems concerning the standards for evaluating it.

Return to the college's system of rewards and punishments. Describing how a record shapes conduct does not by itself tell us whether the practice is fair. A judgement may also require attention to rights, harm and the aims of education. These questions show why the historical explanation of an institution need not exhaust the criticism of that institution.

There is also a practical difficulty in interpreting literature without making the writer its final authority. Biographical documents can clarify the circumstances of writing, and historical evidence may distinguish plausible meanings from anachronistic ones. Anachronism occurs when a later belief is mistakenly attributed to an earlier period. Barthes's argument changes the status of the author's intention, but it does not require the destruction of historical records.

Psychoanalytic criticism faces another problem when it treats literary characters as if they were patients. A character exists through the construction of a work, not through the complete history of a living person. An interpretation must acknowledge that a play provides only selected information about its characters. Lacanian ideas can illuminate patterns of language without supplying direct evidence for a diagnosis.

### What lasted and what came next

These theories helped change the questions that literary critics ask about texts. Foucault's work influenced New Historicism through its attention to relations between cultural practices and power. Kristeva's work on intertextuality and signification informed literary studies and feminist theory. Lacan's account of desire became an important resource for psychoanalytic criticism.

The work of Jacques Derrida developed another major challenge to stable structures of meaning, usually discussed under deconstruction. Its close engagement with conceptual oppositions deserves separate attention. Post-structuralism should therefore not be treated as simply another name for deconstruction. The four approaches studied here also address problems of authorship, institutions, other texts and the formation of the speaking subject.

### How to read these thinkers

A useful order is to begin with Barthes's short essays on authorship and the text. Foucault's lecture on the author then reveals how attribution works as a practice rather than simply a personal fact. Kristeva's essay on Bakhtin extends the investigation towards the relationships among texts. Lacan's mirror-stage essay introduces the problem of a self shaped through its relations to images and language.

The reader can now return to the certificate lying on the college desk. It has not physically changed, yet its place within the story has become richer through these questions. The certificate belongs to an institution, carries inherited meanings and can become an object of identification. What seemed at first to be a simple choice between obedience and disobedience becomes an inquiry into how meaning and identity are formed.

### Important points to remember

- Habermas discusses difficulties in critiques of modern reason in his 1985 book.
- Fraser's 1981 essay questions the normative basis of Foucault's critique of power.
- Describing disciplinary practices does not itself establish whether they are just.
- Historical evidence can help evaluate literary interpretations.
- Foucault influenced New Historicism and Kristeva influenced feminist criticism.
- Deconstruction is a related approach, not a synonym for all post-structuralism.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is post-structuralism in literary theory?**
It is a broad approach that questions fixed meanings, the authority of the author and the idea of a fully independent subject. It developed through debates about structuralism, language, philosophy and psychoanalysis.

**What is the difference between structuralism and post-structuralism?**
Structuralism studies the systems of relations that make signs and texts intelligible. Post-structuralism retains attention to these relations but questions their stability and ability to produce a complete explanation.

**Why is Barthes's The Death of the Author dated both 1967 and 1968?**
The English version first appeared in *Aspen* in 1967, while the French version appeared in *Mantéia* in 1968. The title challenges the author's authority over interpretation rather than referring to a physical death.

**What is the difference between discourse and episteme in Foucault?**
Discourse refers to historically organised practices that form objects of knowledge. An episteme concerns the historical relations among such practices that condition different fields of knowledge.

**What does Julia Kristeva mean by intertextuality?**
Intertextuality is the interaction and transformation of texts within other texts. It draws on Bakhtin's account of dialogue and extends beyond the conscious quotation of an earlier work.

**What are the Imaginary, Symbolic and Real in Lacan's theory?**
The Imaginary concerns images and identification. The Symbolic concerns language, law and social relations. The Real is what resists full symbolisation. They interact rather than forming three separate chronological stages.
