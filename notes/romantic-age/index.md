In 1798, a thin book of poems appeared in Bristol with no author's name on its title page. It was called *Lyrical Ballads*. Two young friends, William Wordsworth and Samuel Taylor Coleridge, had written it, and a bookseller named Joseph Cottle published it. The book did not sell quickly, and the first reviewers were divided. Yet later readers chose it as the starting point of a new age in English writing.

The age closes in 1837, the year Victoria became queen. Between these two dates lay almost forty years of war with France, the rise of the first great factories, and bitter quarrels about who should have a vote. The writers of these years did not stand outside that storm. Many of them had been thrilled by the French Revolution of 1789 before they wrote a line, and what became of that hope shaped much of what they wrote.

The name "Romantic" came later. The writers did not use it as the name of a group, and they often disagreed with one another. Wordsworth looked to the quiet countryside, Byron to the restless life of Europe, Shelley to a future that had not yet arrived, and Keats to beauty and the sadness that comes with it. What joined them was a changed idea of what writing was for. The Augustan writers of the century before, such as Alexander Pope, had prized order, reason and good manners. The new writers prized feeling, imagination and the view of the single person. That change grew out of a world that had been shaken nine years before the book appeared.

## The world of this age

### Political life

The story begins in France. In July 1789 a Paris crowd stormed the Bastille, and the old monarchy began to fall. Many young people in Britain greeted the news with joy. Wordsworth, then a student at Cambridge, later crossed the Channel and lived in France from late 1791 to the end of 1792. He saw ordinary people hoping for a fairer world, and he made that hope his own.

Not everyone shared the joy. Edmund Burke wrote *Reflections on the Revolution in France* in 1790 to warn that sudden change would end in disaster. Thomas Paine answered with *Rights of Man* in 1791 and 1792, arguing that people had a right to choose their own government. The quarrel between these two books divided British readers for years.

Then the Revolution turned violent. The French king was executed in January 1793, and Britain went to war with France a few weeks later. The government of the Prime Minister, William Pitt, feared that revolutionary ideas would spread at home. It suspended habeas corpus in 1794, so that suspects could be held without trial, and in 1795 it passed laws against meetings that it judged seditious. Writers who had cheered the Revolution now had to be careful. In 1798 Coleridge wrote an ode in which he gave up his hope in France, after French armies invaded Switzerland. Wordsworth, too, slowly changed from a friend of the Revolution into a defender of England.

The war went on, with short pauses, until 1815. Napoleon Bonaparte took power in France in 1799 and crowned himself emperor in 1804. The British fleet blocked his plans to invade, most famously when Nelson won the Battle of Trafalgar in 1805. Napoleon met his final defeat at Waterloo in June 1815. During these years the old king, George III, suffered fits of mental illness. In 1811 his son was made Prince Regent and ruled in his name until the king died in 1820. George IV then reigned until 1830, and William IV until 1837.

Peace brought new trouble at home. Thousands of soldiers returned to a country with few jobs, and the Corn Laws of 1815 kept the price of bread high to protect landowners. Hunger and anger grew, and working people began to demand a vote. On 16 August 1819 a crowd of about sixty thousand gathered at St Peter's Field in Manchester to hear a speaker on reform. Cavalry charged into the crowd with drawn sabres. At least fifteen people were killed and hundreds were wounded. The event was nicknamed Peterloo, a bitter joke on the name of Waterloo. Shelley was living in Italy when the news reached him, and it moved him to write *The Masque of Anarchy*, a poem of protest.

Slowly the pressure for change worked. Britain had ended its part in the slave trade in 1807, and slavery itself was abolished across the empire by a law of 1833. Parliament opened its doors to Catholics in 1829. The Reform Act of 1832 swept away many "rotten boroughs", which were tiny seats with almost no voters, and gave seats to growing towns such as Manchester and Leeds. By the time Victoria came to the throne in 1837, the country was calmer, though still divided.

### Social life

When the century turned, Britain counted its people for the first time. The census of 1801 found about nine million people in England and Wales. Thirty years later the figure was nearly fourteen million. Growth of this kind changed daily life. Young men and women left villages that had barely changed for centuries and went to work in towns such as Manchester, Birmingham and Leeds. London was already the largest city in Europe.

Society was sharply divided by rank. At the top stood the nobles and landowners, whose wealth and power came from the land. Below them a growing middle class of merchants, lawyers, clergymen and factory owners wanted a larger say in the nation's affairs. At the bottom were farm labourers and mill workers, who had little say in how they were governed.

A bigger reading public grew up in these years. Circulating libraries lent novels for a small fee, and magazines carried essays, poems and reviews. The *Edinburgh Review* began in 1802, the *Quarterly Review* in 1809 and *Blackwood's Magazine* in 1817. More writers now hoped to earn their living from readers rather than from rich patrons.

Women's lives were narrow. A married woman had few property rights, because the law placed her possessions under her husband's control. Mary Wollstonecraft's *A Vindication of the Rights of Woman* (1792) had asked that girls receive the same education as boys, but change came slowly. Even so, women wrote more than ever. Jane Austen published her novels without her name, and Mary Shelley's *Frankenstein* also came out anonymously.

### Economic life

Britain's wealth in these years rested on land and on machines. On the land, owners were fencing off open fields and commons to farm them more profitably. A general Act of 1801 made this "enclosure" easier. Small farmers and cottagers who had grazed their animals on common land often lost it. John Clare, the son of a farm labourer in Northamptonshire, watched the fields around his village being fenced and wrote sadly about the loss in his poems.

Machines changed work in the towns. Spinning and weaving had once been done by hand, often in workers' homes. Now mills driven by water and steam could do the work faster with fewer hands. Skilled weavers and stocking-knitters in the Midlands and the north saw their wages fall. In 1811 groups of workers in Nottinghamshire began to smash the new knitting frames at night. They called themselves Luddites, after a legendary leader named Ned Ludd. In 1812 Parliament made frame-breaking a crime punishable by death. Byron, a lord by inheritance, spoke against the bill in his first speech in the House of Lords, in February 1812. The bill became law all the same. In January 1813, after a wave of attacks on mills and masters, a special court at York hanged seventeen men.

Children worked in mills and mines, often for twelve hours or more. Earlier laws to protect them had been weak, and the Factory Act of 1833 was the first to be enforced by government inspectors. Blake, who lived in London, saw the misery of children in its streets, and he put it into poems such as "The Chimney Sweeper", which appears in both of his song books.

Travel changed too. Canals carried heavy goods across the country, and then came the railway. The Stockton and Darlington line opened in 1825, and in 1830 the Liverpool and Manchester Railway began to carry passengers by steam between two great cities. Many poets viewed these changes with unease, and Wordsworth later spoke out against a railway planned for his beloved Lake District.

### Religion

The Church of England stood at the centre of national life. Its bishops sat in the House of Lords, and its clergy served in every parish. Outside it, Methodism, the movement begun by John Wesley, had spread among working people, and Dissenting groups such as the Unitarians held other beliefs. Dissenters and Catholics were shut out of many public posts. This began to change only with the repeal of the Test and Corporation Acts in 1828 and Catholic emancipation in 1829.

At the same time, doubt was growing. Thomas Paine's *The Age of Reason* (1794) attacked the claim that the Bible was a revealed book. Percy Shelley, a student at Oxford, printed a pamphlet called *The Necessity of Atheism* in 1811 and was expelled from the university. Coleridge's path was different. In the 1790s he preached in Unitarian chapels, but in later life he returned to the Church of England and wrote a great deal about Christian thought.

The poets of the age searched for the divine in new places. Blake, a devout but unusual Christian, built a mythology of his own. Wordsworth found in nature a living presence that could calm and teach the human heart. Shelley openly rejected organised religion, yet in 1816 he wrote a "Hymn to Intellectual Beauty", praising a spirit of beauty that he could not name.

### Did you know?

On 11 May 1812, Prime Minister Spencer Perceval was shot dead in the lobby of the House of Commons by John Bellingham, a bankrupt merchant with a private grudge against the government. He remains the only British prime minister ever to have been assassinated. Bellingham was tried within a week and hanged soon afterwards.

### Important points to remember

- The French Revolution of 1789 thrilled many young British writers, but its violence and the war that followed made many of them change their minds.
- Britain was at war with France from 1793 to 1815, and Napoleon was beaten at Waterloo in June 1815.
- Peterloo (16 August 1819) was a cavalry charge on a reform meeting in Manchester, and Shelley answered it with *The Masque of Anarchy*.
- The slave trade ended in 1807, Catholics could sit in Parliament from 1829, the Reform Act came in 1832, and slavery was abolished in 1833.
- The first census, in 1801, counted about nine million people in England and Wales, and by 1831 the figure was nearly fourteen million.
- The Luddites smashed knitting frames from 1811, and Byron spoke against the bill that made frame-breaking a hanging offence.
- The *Edinburgh Review* (1802), the *Quarterly Review* (1809) and *Blackwood's Magazine* (1817) shaped what the public read.
- Shelley was expelled from Oxford in 1811 for *The Necessity of Atheism*.

## Literature in this age

### The language

Poetry in 1790 still spoke in the voice of the eighteenth century. Alexander Pope and Samuel Johnson had written in balanced couplets and in an elegant, raised style. Poets of that school liked roundabout phrases better than plain nouns, and this habit was later called poetic diction. A few writers had already begun to tire of it. Thomas Gray wrote about the graves of humble villagers in 1751, and Robert Burns used the speech of Scotland in his poems of 1786.

The decisive step came in Somerset in 1797. Wordsworth, with his sister Dorothy, rented Alfoxden House, a few miles from the village of Nether Stowey, where Coleridge lived. The two poets walked and talked almost every day, and out of those talks came the plan for *Lyrical Ballads*. Coleridge later recalled the plan in *Biographia Literaria*. He would write about strange and supernatural events and make them feel true, by winning from the reader what he called a willing suspension of disbelief. Wordsworth would take ordinary people and everyday scenes and show the wonder hidden in them.

The 1798 book opened with Coleridge's *The Rime of the Ancient Mariner* and closed with Wordsworth's "Lines Written a Few Miles above Tintern Abbey". Between them stood poems about a little girl who would not accept that two of her brothers and sisters had died, and about other humble people. When the second edition came out in 1800, Wordsworth added a long Preface to explain what the two friends were trying to do. He wrote that poetry should use the language really used by men, that good poetry is the "spontaneous overflow of powerful feelings", and that it takes its origin from "emotion recollected in tranquillity". In other words, a poem begins in strong feeling, but the poet shapes it afterwards in a calm mind.

Not everyone agreed with him. Coleridge himself, in *Biographia Literaria* (1817), argued that Wordsworth's rule about the speech of country people was too narrow, because great poetry could not simply copy the talk of villagers.

### How the writing reached its readers

A writer of this age lived by publishers, magazines and reviewers. John Murray of London published Byron's poems and Jane Austen's *Emma*. A good review could make a name, and a bad one could wound. In 1808 the *Edinburgh Review* attacked *Hours of Idleness*, the first book of the young Byron. He answered in 1809 with a verse satire, *English Bards and Scotch Reviewers*, which mocked the reviewers and many poets of the day, Wordsworth, Coleridge and Southey among them.

In 1814 Francis Jeffrey, the editor of the same review, began his notice of Wordsworth's long poem *The Excursion* by saying that it would never do. In 1818 *Blackwood's Magazine* mocked Keats and his friend Leigh Hunt as members of a "Cockney School" of poetry, sneering at their London, middle-class background. The *Quarterly Review* soon joined in with its own attack on Keats's *Endymion*. Shelley came to believe that these attacks had killed his friend. In truth Keats died of tuberculosis.

Some of the greatest work reached readers late. Wordsworth went on revising *The Prelude* for most of his life, and it was published only after his death in 1850. Shelley's *A Defence of Poetry* was written in 1821 and not printed until 1840. Coleridge's *Kubla Khan* sat in manuscript for years before the public saw it in 1816.

### Poetry

Poetry had been moving towards new ground before 1798. Edmund Burke's essay of 1757 on the sublime and the beautiful had taught readers that vast and wild things, such as mountains and storms, could give a deep, almost fearful pleasure. Thomas Percy's *Reliques of Ancient English Poetry* (1765) brought old ballads back to readers, and James Macpherson's "Ossian" poems of the 1760s offered misty tales of an ancient Gaelic bard. Macpherson claimed to have translated them, but they proved to be largely his own work. Robert Burns, a farmer's son from Ayrshire, died in 1796, having shown that fine poetry could come from a ploughman's pen.

William Blake stood apart from all of them. A London engraver, he printed his words and pictures together from copper plates that he etched himself and then coloured each page by hand. This method is called illuminated printing. *Songs of Innocence* (1789) looked at the world as a child sees it, and *Songs of Experience* (1794) looked at the same world after cruelty and injustice have been seen. His longer "prophetic books" told of giant figures in a mythology that he invented. Few people bought his work. A soldier once accused him of speaking against the king, and in 1804 a jury found him not guilty. He died in 1827 with little fame.

Wordsworth grew up among the hills and lakes of northern England, and the memory of that landscape never left him. In December 1799 he and his sister Dorothy settled at Dove Cottage in Grasmere, in the Lake District. Dorothy kept journals in which she noted flowers, weather and passers-by, and William often turned her notes into poems. In April 1802 the two saw a long line of daffodils beside Ullswater, and Dorothy described the sight in her journal. Two years later it became "I Wandered Lonely as a Cloud". His greatest work was *The Prelude*, a long blank-verse poem addressed to Coleridge about the growth of his own mind. He began it in 1798 and first completed it in 1805. In it he looked back on his childhood, his years at Cambridge and his time in revolutionary France.

Coleridge was the thinker of the group. He was brilliant and restless, often unwell, and he took opium, then sold freely as a medicine, to ease his pain and came to depend on it. His best-known poems were written quickly. *The Rime of the Ancient Mariner* (1798) tells of a sailor who shoots an albatross and brings a curse on his ship, and who must afterwards tell his story to strangers for the rest of his life. *Kubla Khan* and *Christabel*, a tale of a lady, a stranger and magic in a castle, were published in 1816 as fragments. By Coleridge's own account, he dreamed the lines of *Kubla Khan* after taking opium, and forgot the rest when a visitor from Porlock interrupted his writing. In 1800 he moved to Keswick, and Robert Southey joined him there in 1803. Wordsworth, Coleridge and Southey came to be called the "Lake Poets", a label first given by hostile critics. Southey wrote long narrative poems and a *Life of Nelson* (1813), and he became Poet Laureate in 1813 after Walter Scott turned the post down.

Scott, an Edinburgh lawyer, was the best-selling poet of those years. His long verse tales set in medieval Scotland, *The Lay of the Last Minstrel* (1805), *Marmion* (1808) and *The Lady of the Lake* (1810), were read all over the country.

A younger group came forward after 1810. Byron was born in 1788, Shelley in 1792 and Keats in 1795. They were children when the Revolution began and grew up during the long war. Some of them felt that the older poets, now settled and respectable, had turned their backs on the causes of their youth. Byron's *Don Juan* opens with a mocking dedication to Southey, and in 1821 Southey hit back by calling Byron and writers like him the "Satanic School", because he thought them rebellious and immoral.

George Gordon, Lord Byron, inherited his title at the age of ten. After the reviewers mocked his first book, he travelled through Portugal, Spain, Malta, Albania, Greece and Turkey from 1809 to 1811. The first two cantos of *Childe Harold's Pilgrimage*, about a gloomy young wanderer in those lands, appeared in March 1812. The first edition sold out within days, and Byron, as he later put it, woke up one morning and found himself famous. London society adored him. The proud, moody wanderer of his poems gave the world a figure later called the Byronic hero. Then his life fell apart. He married Annabella Milbanke in January 1815, but the marriage failed within a year, and rumour and scandal followed him. In April 1816 he left England and never returned. In exile he wrote *Manfred* (1817), about a proud man tortured by secret guilt, and *Don Juan* (1819-24), a long comic poem in which a young man passes through love affairs and wars across Europe while the poet pokes fun at hypocrisy and war. Byron left it unfinished. In 1823 he sailed to Greece to help its war of independence against the Ottoman Empire, and he died of a fever at Missolonghi on 19 April 1824, aged thirty-six.

Percy Bysshe Shelley was born in 1792 into a wealthy family. After his expulsion from Oxford he lived as a radical. He admired William Godwin, the author of *Political Justice* (1793), and in 1814 he ran away with Godwin's daughter Mary. They married in December 1816, and in 1818 they left England for Italy for good. There he wrote with great speed. *Prometheus Unbound* (1820) is a poem in the form of a play in which the Titan who gave fire to humans and was punished by Zeus stands for humanity's struggle against tyranny. In "Ode to the West Wind" (1819) he asks the wind to carry his ideas across the world, and *Adonais* (1821) mourns the death of Keats. On 8 July 1822, when he was not yet thirty, Shelley drowned when his small sailing boat was caught in a storm in the Gulf of Spezia. His body was burned on the beach, and his ashes lie in the Protestant Cemetery in Rome, close to the grave of Keats.

John Keats was born in London in 1795, the son of a man who managed a stable. He lost his father at the age of eight and his mother at fourteen. He trained as a surgeon at Guy's Hospital but gave up medicine to write poetry. His friend Leigh Hunt, the editor of the *Examiner*, encouraged him and printed his early poems. *Poems* (1817) was his first book, and *Endymion* (1818), a long poem on a Greek myth, brought him the attacks of the reviewers. His brother Tom, whom he nursed, died of tuberculosis in December 1818. In the months that followed, in 1819, Keats wrote the poems on which his fame rests: the romance *The Eve of St Agnes*, the great odes, among them "Ode to a Nightingale" and "Ode on a Grecian Urn", and the autumn poem "To Autumn". Meanwhile he had fallen in love with Fanny Brawne and had begun to show signs of the disease that had killed his mother and brother. Early in 1820 he coughed blood. Doctors advised a winter in the warm south, and in September 1820 he sailed for Italy with his friend, the painter Joseph Severn. Keats believed that a great poet must be able to stay in doubt and mystery without hurrying towards certainty, a power he named negative capability in a letter of December 1817. He died in Rome on 23 February 1821, aged twenty-five. He asked that no name be put on his gravestone, only words saying that his name was written in water.

Other voices were heard as well. John Clare (1793-1864) wrote of village life, birds and fields. *Poems Descriptive of Rural Life and Scenery* (1820) won him praise, but he later fell into poverty and mental illness. Felicia Hemans (1793-1835) became one of the most widely read poets of the day with poems on home, war and women's lives, and her *Records of Woman* appeared in 1828.

### Prose

Prose fiction in the 1790s was already filling with mystery and terror. Horace Walpole's *The Castle of Otranto* (1764), a tale of a haunted castle, had started a fashion. Ann Radcliffe's *The Mysteries of Udolpho* (1794) added wild mountain scenery and long, tense suspense, and Matthew Lewis's *The Monk* (1796) was so dark that it shocked many readers. This kind of tale became known as the Gothic novel.

Jane Austen read these books and laughed at them. In *Northanger Abbey*, a young woman who has read too many Gothic tales imagines horrors in a quiet country house. Austen was born in a Hampshire village in 1775 and wrote about families, marriage and money in small country circles. *Sense and Sensibility* (1811) was published "By a Lady", and *Pride and Prejudice* (1813), *Mansfield Park* (1814) and *Emma* (1815) followed. She died in 1817, aged forty-one, and *Northanger Abbey* and *Persuasion* appeared after her death, in December of that year.

Walter Scott had been the most popular poet in Britain until Byron's poems began to outsell his own. Around 1814 he turned to prose. *Waverley* (1814) follows a young English officer who is drawn into the Jacobite rising of 1745 in Scotland. It appeared without Scott's name, and for years his novels were credited to "the author of *Waverley*". He admitted in 1827 that he was that author. By mixing invented characters with real events, Scott created the historical novel, and writers all over Europe followed him. *Ivanhoe* (1819) carried the form back to medieval England.

The Gothic tradition found its most famous form in *Frankenstein; or, The Modern Prometheus* (1818) by Mary Shelley. Its hero, Victor Frankenstein, is a young scientist who builds a living creature from dead matter and then runs away from what he has made. The book mixes Gothic horror with real questions about the limits of science, which had become a lively subject in an age of new discoveries. It also came out without the author's name.

The magazines gave a home to the essay. Charles Lamb, a clerk at the East India Company, wrote essays for the *London Magazine* under the name Elia, in a warm, joking, personal voice, and they were collected as *Essays of Elia* in 1823. William Hazlitt wrote sharp, vivid essays and portraits, among them *The Spirit of the Age* (1825). Thomas De Quincey's *Confessions of an English Opium-Eater* (1821) told, with dream and detail, the story of his own addiction. Thomas Love Peacock mocked the poets in comic novels such as *Nightmare Abbey* (1818).

The theatre gave the age less. Only a few London houses, chiefly Drury Lane and Covent Garden, were licensed to stage spoken drama, and their huge halls favoured spectacle and melodrama. The great poets wrote verse plays that were meant to be read rather than staged, which is why such works are called closet drama. Byron's *Manfred* (1817) and Shelley's *Prometheus Unbound* (1820) are examples. The one great star of the stage was the actor Edmund Kean, who made his name at Drury Lane in 1814 as Shylock.

### An interesting story

In June 1816, Byron rented the Villa Diodati near Lake Geneva. He had left England in April, after his marriage ended in scandal. Percy Shelley, Mary Godwin, who was eighteen, and Byron's doctor, John Polidori, stayed close by. The summer was cold and wet across Europe, because Mount Tambora in Indonesia had erupted the year before, so the friends spent many evenings indoors. They read German ghost stories in a French translation, and Byron suggested that each of them write one. Mary had no idea at first. Days later, half awake, she saw in her mind a student of science kneeling beside the creature he had made. That vision grew into *Frankenstein*, published in 1818. Polidori's tale *The Vampyre* appeared in 1819, at first under Byron's name.

### Key terms

- **Romanticism** - a movement in writing, art and music, from about the 1780s to the 1830s, that valued feeling, imagination, nature and the view of the single person.
- **Lyrical Ballads** - the book of poems by Wordsworth and Coleridge (1798) that is taken as the start of the age.
- **Poetic diction** - the raised, roundabout language that eighteenth-century poets used in place of plain words.
- **Imagination** - for Coleridge, the power of the mind that fuses separate things into a new whole.
- **Fancy** - for Coleridge, a lower power that only rearranges ready-made images.
- **Willing suspension of disbelief** - Coleridge's phrase for the reader's readiness to accept a supernatural tale for the moment.
- **Spontaneous overflow of powerful feelings** - Wordsworth's description of how poetry begins.
- **Emotion recollected in tranquillity** - Wordsworth's phrase for the calm remembering that shapes strong feeling into a poem.
- **Sublime** - the feeling of awe, and even fear, that vast or wild things such as mountains and storms can give; explained by Edmund Burke in 1757.
- **Negative capability** - Keats's name for the power to stay in doubt and mystery without hurrying towards certain answers.
- **Lake Poets** - Wordsworth, Coleridge and Southey, who lived in or near the Lake District.
- **Byronic hero** - a proud, moody and rebellious outsider with a secret guilt, drawn from Byron's poems and from Byron himself.
- **Satanic School** - Robert Southey's insulting name for Byron and writers like him, whom he thought rebellious and immoral.
- **Gothic novel** - a tale of mystery and terror, set among old castles, secrets and ghostly happenings.
- **Historical novel** - a story in which invented characters live through real events of the past; Scott's *Waverley* is the model.
- **Closet drama** - a play written to be read rather than staged.
- **Illuminated printing** - Blake's method of printing words and pictures together from etched plates and colouring them by hand.
- **Elegy** - a poem of mourning; Shelley's *Adonais* mourns Keats.

### Criticism and scholarship

The Romantics were also their own critics. Wordsworth's Preface of 1800 and Coleridge's *Biographia Literaria* of 1817 set out their ideas in plain argument. In *Biographia Literaria* Coleridge also separated two powers of the mind. Fancy, he said, only rearranges ready-made images, while imagination fuses them into something new. William Hazlitt judged his fellow writers in *The Spirit of the Age* (1825). In 1820 Thomas Love Peacock wrote a playful essay, *The Four Ages of Poetry*, which said that poetry had lost its use in a scientific age. Shelley answered it with *A Defence of Poetry*, written in 1821, in which he called poets the "unacknowledged legislators of the world".

Scholars of the twentieth century argued about what the word "Romantic" meant. In 1924 Arthur O. Lovejoy argued in "On the Discrimination of Romanticisms" that the word covered so many different ideas that it should be used in the plural. In 1949 René Wellek replied that the poets did share a common view of imagination, nature and symbol. M. H. Abrams, in *The Mirror and the Lamp* (1953), showed how criticism moved from the idea that art holds a mirror up to nature to the idea that art is a lamp that shines from the poet's own mind. His *Natural Supernaturalism* (1971) argued that the Romantics gave old religious ideas of fall, struggle and recovery a new form in the story of the human mind.

More recent scholars looked again at history and gender. Marilyn Butler's *Romantics, Rebels and Reactionaries* (1981) set the writers among the political quarrels of their day. Jerome McGann's *The Romantic Ideology* (1983) argued that critics had too often accepted the poets' own picture of themselves, and urged readers to ask what the poems leave out. Anne K. Mellor's *Romanticism and Gender* (1993) asked why women writers had been missing from the old picture of the age.

### Important points to remember

- *Lyrical Ballads* (1798) by Wordsworth and Coleridge marks the start of the age. It opened with *The Rime of the Ancient Mariner* and closed with "Tintern Abbey".
- Wordsworth's Preface (1800) says that poetry should use the language really used by men, and that it is the spontaneous overflow of powerful feelings recollected in tranquillity.
- Coleridge's *Biographia Literaria* (1817) is the main critical book of the age, and it also questions Wordsworth's rule about the speech of country people.
- The older poets are Blake, Wordsworth, Coleridge, Southey and Scott. The younger poets are Byron, Shelley and Keats.
- Byron: *Childe Harold's Pilgrimage* (cantos I and II in 1812), *Don Juan* (1819-24), and death at Missolonghi in 1824.
- Shelley: *Prometheus Unbound* (1820), *Adonais* (1821), and drowning in 1822.
- Keats: *Endymion* (1818), the great odes of 1819, and death in Rome in 1821, aged twenty-five.
- Blake: *Songs of Innocence* (1789) and *Songs of Experience* (1794), made by illuminated printing.
- Prose fiction: Austen's novels (1811-17), Scott's *Waverley* (1814), the first historical novel, and Mary Shelley's Gothic *Frankenstein* (1818).
- Essay: Lamb's *Essays of Elia*, Hazlitt's *The Spirit of the Age*, and De Quincey's *Confessions of an English Opium-Eater*.

## How the age ends

The age did not end in a single day. The poets who had opened it were passing away, one after another. Keats died in Rome in 1821, Shelley drowned in 1822 and Byron died in Greece in 1824. Blake died in 1827. Scott, worn out by years of writing to pay off the debts of the firms that failed in 1826, died in 1832. Coleridge and Lamb died in 1834, and Hemans in 1835. Wordsworth lived on until 1850. The poet who had once startled readers by using the speech of ordinary people became a respected old man and, in 1843, Poet Laureate.

Britain, too, was changing. The Reform Act of 1832 had given the new towns a voice. In 1833 came the Factory Act and the abolition of slavery in the empire. The railways were beginning to cross the country. In that same year, 1833, a movement began at Oxford that asked the Church of England to recover its older traditions. It came to be called the Oxford Movement.

King William IV died in June 1837, and his eighteen-year-old niece Victoria became queen on 20 June. In those same months Charles Dickens was finishing *The Pickwick Papers*, which had been appearing in monthly parts since 1836, and he began *Oliver Twist*. A new kind of reader and writer, urban and middle class, was coming forward. John Clare, whose village poems had once been praised, entered an asylum in Essex that year and spent the rest of his life in asylums.

The date 1837 is a convention. Some literary historians prefer to end the age in 1832, the year of the Reform Act and of Scott's death. In either case the ideas of the Romantics did not vanish. They passed to the next generation. Alfred Tennyson, then a boy of fourteen, was so struck by the news of Byron's death in 1824 that he carved the words "Byron is dead" on a rock. He would grow into one of the leading poets of the Victorian Age.

### Important points to remember

- The age is dated from 1798, the year of *Lyrical Ballads*, to 1837, the year Victoria became queen. Some historians end it in 1832.
- Keats died in 1821, Shelley in 1822, Byron in 1824, Blake in 1827, Scott in 1832, and Coleridge and Lamb in 1834.
- Wordsworth lived until 1850, and he became Poet Laureate in 1843.
- The Reform Act came in 1832, and the Factory Act and the abolition of slavery in 1833. The Oxford Movement also began in 1833.
- Victoria became queen on 20 June 1837, at the age of eighteen.
- Dickens's *The Pickwick Papers* (1836-37) and *Oliver Twist* (from 1837) belong to the new age.
- John Clare entered an asylum in 1837 and stayed in asylums for the rest of his life.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why does the Romantic Age begin in 1798?**
That is the year *Lyrical Ballads*, the joint book of Wordsworth and Coleridge, was published. It brought plain speech, ordinary people and the supernatural into poetry in a new way, and later readers took it as the first clear sign of a new age.

**Why does the age end in 1837?**
That was the year Victoria became queen, and the Victorian Age is counted from it. Some historians prefer to end the Romantic Age in 1832, the year of the Reform Act and of Scott's death.

**Who are the main poets of the Romantic Age?**
The older group is Blake, Wordsworth, Coleridge, Southey and Scott. The younger group is Byron, Shelley and Keats. John Clare and Felicia Hemans are also read today.

**What was the plan behind *Lyrical Ballads*?**
By Coleridge's account, he would write about supernatural events and make them seem true, while Wordsworth would write about ordinary people and everyday life and show the wonder hidden in them.

**Which novelists belong to the Romantic Age?**
Jane Austen, Walter Scott and Mary Shelley are the main names. Austen wrote about country families, Scott created the historical novel, and Mary Shelley wrote the Gothic novel *Frankenstein*. Thomas Love Peacock wrote comic novels that made fun of the poets.

**How did the French Revolution affect Romantic writers?**
It filled many young writers with hope for a fairer world. When the Revolution turned violent and war began, Wordsworth and Coleridge lost their hope in France. Younger poets such as Shelley and Byron went on speaking for freedom in their writing and in their lives.
