Picture Victorian Britain in the nineteenth century. Factory chimneys darken the sky, railways cut across quiet valleys, and new scientific discoveries unsettle old religious certainties. Amid this rapid change, critics ask an urgent question: what is art for in an industrial world? Their answers divide the age into two great camps. One camp insists that literature and art must serve morality, truth and social health. The other answers that art owes nothing to sermons or factories—it exists purely for the sake of beauty.

Four names stand for this debate. Matthew Arnold called poetry "a criticism of life" and looked to high culture to steady a restless society. John Ruskin tied great art to moral truthfulness and the free, imperfect hand of the craftsman. Walter Pater shifted criticism toward Aestheticism, urging readers to savour intense beauty and live with a "hard, gem-like flame". Oscar Wilde pushed Aestheticism to its wittiest extreme, declaring that "All art is quite useless" and that the critic himself is an artist.

This is the sixth article in the series on the history of English literary criticism. In the previous article, we saw Wordsworth, Coleridge, Shelley and Hazlitt place imagination and feeling at the centre of poetry. Victorian critics kept the Romantic reverence for art, yet argued fiercely over whether art's true home is public duty or private beauty—a quarrel that leads straight into twentieth-century Modernism.

## The world of Victorian criticism

### Moral duty versus art for art's sake

Across Queen Victoria's long reign (1837-1901), English criticism swung like a pendulum between two poles. In the early and mid-Victorian years, critics like Matthew Arnold and John Ruskin felt a heavy sense of public responsibility. They wanted literature and art to educate the growing middle class, elevate taste and resist the ugliness of industrial capitalism.

By the 1870s and 1890s, a younger generation grew weary of earnest Victorian moralising. Influenced by French writers such as Théophile Gautier and the slogan *l'art pour l'art* ("art for art's sake"), Walter Pater and Oscar Wilde championed the **Aesthetic Movement**. They argued that judging a poem or painting by its moral lesson is a mistake: art has its own laws, and beauty is its own justification.

### Science, industry and the crisis of faith

Victorian criticism cannot be separated from the anxieties of its time. Works such as Charles Darwin's *On the Origin of Species* (1859) shook traditional religious belief, while rapid urbanisation and mass journalism flooded the market with cheap, hurried writing. Arnold feared that as formal religion weakened, society would lose its moral anchor unless poetry and culture stepped forward to "interpret life for us, to console us, to sustain us".

### A map of the age

In Abrams's map of critical theories, Victorian criticism splits cleanly down the middle. Arnold and Ruskin lean toward the **pragmatic** and moral view, measuring art by its civilising power and truthfulness to life. Pater and Wilde lean toward **impressionistic** and **aesthetic** criticism, treating the artwork as an autonomous world of beauty—a stance that helped prepare the ground for twentieth-century Formalism and New Criticism.

### Did you know?

Walter Pater was so unsettled when conservative Oxford critics accused the famous Conclusion of *Studies in the History of the Renaissance* (1873) of promoting selfish pleasure-seeking that he quietly removed the entire Conclusion from the second edition of 1877. He only restored it in the third edition of 1888, with a brief note explaining his true meaning.

### Important points to remember

- Victorian criticism (1837-1901) was shaped by a central debate between moral/social responsibility and "art for art's sake".
- Matthew Arnold and John Ruskin represent the moral and cultural tradition; Walter Pater and Oscar Wilde represent the Aesthetic Movement.
- Industrialisation, mass journalism and the crisis of religious faith gave Victorian criticism an urgent public tone.
- In Abrams's map, Arnold and Ruskin lean toward the pragmatic view, while Pater and Wilde move toward aesthetic autonomy and impressionism.
- Walter Pater omitted the famous Conclusion from the 1877 second edition of *The Renaissance* out of fear it might mislead young readers, restoring it in 1888.

## Matthew Arnold: culture and the touchstone

Matthew Arnold (1822-1888)—poet, inspector of schools and Professor of Poetry at Oxford—was the dominant literary critic of the Victorian era. His most influential critical writings include the *Preface to Poems* (1853), *Essays in Criticism* (First Series, 1865; Second Series, 1888), *Culture and Anarchy* (1869), and his landmark essay "The Study of Poetry" (1880).

### Disinterestedness and the function of criticism

In "The Function of Criticism at the Present Time" (published in *Essays in Criticism*, 1865), Arnold gave criticism an ambitious cultural mission. He defined criticism as:

> "A disinterested endeavour to learn and propagate the best that is known and thought in the world."

By **disinterestedness**, Arnold did not mean indifference or boredom. He meant keeping the mind free from party politics, personal vanity and narrow commercial interests so that the critic can "see the object as in itself it really is". Only by circulating fresh, European ideas—not merely provincial English ones—could criticism prepare the soil for great creative literature.

In *Culture and Anarchy* (1869), Arnold widened this critique to society at large. Borrowing a phrase from Jonathan Swift, he described culture as **"sweetness and light"** (beauty and intelligence). He famously divided Victorian society into three classes—the **Barbarians** (aristocracy), the **Philistines** (materialistic middle class) and the **Populace** (working class)—and argued that England suffered from too much narrow moral strictness (**Hebraism**) and too little open-minded curiosity (**Hellenism**).

### Poetry as "a criticism of life"

In "The Study of Poetry" (written in 1880 as the general introduction to T. H. Ward's anthology *The English Poets*, and reprinted in *Essays in Criticism*, Second Series, 1888), Arnold set out his mature view of poetry. Poetry, he declared, is "a criticism of life under the conditions fixed for such a criticism by the laws of poetic truth and poetic beauty".

Great poetry, Arnold argued, does not merely entertain; it illuminates how to live. To reach the highest rank, a poem must unite superior style—what Arnold called the **grand style**—with **high seriousness**, an idea he adapted from Aristotle's *Poetics* (*spoudaiotes*).

### The touchstone method and the three estimates

How should a reader recognise truly great poetry? In "The Study of Poetry", Arnold warned against two common traps:

- **The historical estimate:** overrating a poem simply because it is old or played an important role in the development of the language.
- **The personal estimate:** overrating a poem because of private affections, memories or personal affinities.

Both traps distort judgment. Critics must strive instead for the **real estimate**. To achieve it, Arnold proposed his famous **touchstone method**: keep in mind a few short lines and passages from supreme masters—Homer, Dante, Shakespeare and Milton—and hold other poems beside them, just as a jeweller tests gold against a touchstone.

Applying this strict standard, Arnold delivered famous—and fiercely debated—verdicts. He loved Geoffrey Chaucer's lively humanity, yet denied him the highest rank because he lacked "high seriousness". Even more provocatively, he declared that John Dryden and Alexander Pope were not classics of English poetry at all: "they are classics of our prose".

- Matthew Arnold (1822-1888) was Professor of Poetry at Oxford and the central Victorian literary critic.

- In "The Study of Poetry" (1880), Arnold called poetry "a criticism of life" marked by "high seriousness" and the "grand style".

- His **touchstone method** tests poems against short passages from Homer, Dante, Shakespeare and Milton.
- Arnold said Chaucer lacked "high seriousness" and called Dryden and Pope "classics of our prose".

## John Ruskin: morality, nature and craft

John Ruskin (1819-1900) was the most influential art and social critic of Victorian Britain. Although he began by defending the painter J. M. W. Turner, his passionate prose reshaped how Victorians thought about painting, architecture, labour and literature alike. His major works include *Modern Painters* (five volumes, 1843-1860), *The Seven Lamps of Architecture* (1849), *The Stones of Venice* (three volumes, 1851-1853), and *Unto This Last* (1860; book form 1862).

### Art as a moral force

For Ruskin, art and morality are inseparable. Great art is never a shallow decorative trick; it is the expression of a truthful, reverent mind and a just society. He hated the soulless uniformity of industrial factories, which turned human workers into mere machines.

In his celebrated chapter "The Nature of Gothic" (*The Stones of Venice*, Volume 2, 1853), Ruskin praised medieval Gothic cathedrals precisely because their carved stone showed the free, irregular touch of individual craftsmen. As he declared, "neither architecture nor any other noble work of man can be good unless it be imperfect", for imperfection is the sign of living human effort rather than mechanical slavery.

### The pathetic fallacy

Ruskin's most famous contribution to literary criticism appears in Volume 3, Part 4, Chapter 12 of *Modern Painters* (1856), in a chapter titled **"Of the Pathetic Fallacy"**. Ruskin coined the term **pathetic fallacy** for the poetic habit of attributing human emotions to inanimate objects in nature.

His famous example comes from Charles Kingsley's poem in *Alton Locke*, which describes the sea as "the cruel, crawling foam". Foam, Ruskin points out, is not cruel, nor does it crawl; it is only the speaker's violent grief that projects human feeling onto water. Ruskin distinguished between two orders of poets: the highest, like Homer, see the natural world clearly and steadily even in moments of deep feeling; the second order, swept away by emotion, fall into the pathetic fallacy. Today, critics use the term neutrally as a descriptive label for personified nature, yet its origin lies in Ruskin's fierce devotion to truth.

### Important points to remember

- John Ruskin (1819-1900) was the leading Victorian art and social critic, linking great art directly to moral truth and human dignity.
- His major works include *Modern Painters* (1843-1860), *The Seven Lamps of Architecture* (1849), *The Stones of Venice* (1851-1853) and *Unto This Last* (1860/1862).
- In "The Nature of Gothic" (*The Stones of Venice*), Ruskin praised medieval imperfection as the mark of human freedom over industrial monotony.
- In *Modern Painters*, Volume 3 (1856), Ruskin coined the term **pathetic fallacy** for attributing human feelings to inanimate nature (e.g., "the cruel, crawling foam").
- Ruskin ranked calm, truthful observation of nature above emotional distortion.

## Walter Pater: the Aesthetic turn

Walter Pater (1839-1894), a quiet, scholarly fellow of Brasenose College, Oxford, gently dismantled the moral certainty of mid-Victorian criticism. His slim, exquisite book *Studies in the History of the Renaissance* (1873; revised as *The Renaissance: Studies in Art and Poetry* in 1877) and his later collection *Appreciations, with an Essay on Style* (1889) became the guiding texts of the English **Aesthetic Movement**.

### Impressionistic criticism

In the Preface to *The Renaissance*, Pater quoted Matthew Arnold's famous rule—to see the object "as in itself it really is"—and quietly turned it inside out. In aesthetic criticism, Pater replied, "the first step towards seeing one's object as it really is, is to know one's own impression as it really is".

Instead of asking whether a poem teaches virtue or matches a classical touchstone, Pater's aesthetic critic asks personal, sensory questions: "What is this song or picture, this engaging personality presented in life or in a book, to *me*? What effect does it really produce on me?" Criticism, in Pater's hands, becomes subtle, personal **appreciation**—an effort to catch and describe the unique flavour of each work of art.

### The "hard, gem-like flame" and the condition of music

In the famous **Conclusion** to *The Renaissance* (1873), Pater meditated on the fleetingness of human life. Every moment slips away like water, leaving us only a brief interval of conscious awareness. The wisest response, Pater argued, is to sharpen our senses and live as intensely as possible through art and beauty:

> "To burn always with this hard, gem-like flame, to maintain this ecstasy, is success in life."

Because art comes to us "proposing frankly to give nothing but the highest quality to your moments as they pass, and simply for those moments' sake", Pater closed the book by praising the love of **"art for its own sake"**.

In his essay "The School of Giorgione" (added in 1877), Pater offered another enduring maxim: "All art constantly aspires towards the condition of music." Why music? Because in music, form and content cannot be separated—you cannot summarise a symphony's "message" apart from its notes. Great poetry and painting strive for that same perfect fusion of form and meaning.

### Important points to remember

- Walter Pater (1839-1894) was an Oxford scholar and the intellectual leader of the English Aesthetic Movement.
- His key critical books are *Studies in the History of the Renaissance* (1873; revised 1877) and *Appreciations, with an Essay on Style* (1889).
- Reversing Arnold, Pater argued that the aesthetic critic must first "know one's own impression as it really is".
- In the Conclusion to *The Renaissance*, Pater wrote: "To burn always with this hard, gem-like flame, to maintain this ecstasy, is success in life."
- Pater championed "art for its own sake", valuing art for the intensity it gives to passing moments.
- In "The School of Giorgione" (1877), he declared: "All art constantly aspires towards the condition of music."

## Oscar Wilde: the critic as artist

Oscar Wilde (1854-1900)—dramatist, novelist and dazzling conversationalist—studied at Oxford under both Ruskin and Pater, and turned Pater's quiet aestheticism into bold, provocative paradox. His major critical essays were gathered in *Intentions* (1891), alongside his famous epigrammatic *Preface to The Picture of Dorian Gray* (1891).

### *The Decay of Lying* and the reversal of imitation

First published in 1889 and collected in *Intentions* (1891), **"The Decay of Lying"** is a sparkling dialogue between **Vivian** and **Cyril**. With playful seriousness, Wilde attacks realistic fiction and turns two thousand years of Aristotelian *mimesis* upside down.

Art, Vivian argues, is not a dull photograph of everyday facts; "Lying, the telling of beautiful untrue things, is the proper aim of Art." Even more famously, Wilde reverses the ancient mirror metaphor altogether: "Life imitates Art far more than Art imitates Life." People only began noticing the strange beauty of London fogs, Vivian jokes, after Impressionist painters taught them how to look at fog. Nature itself is shaped by the artistic images through which we learn to see it.

### *The Critic as Artist*

In **"The Critic as Artist"** (published 1890; collected in *Intentions*, 1891)—a two-part dialogue between **Gilbert** and **Ernest**—Wilde makes the boldest claim for literary criticism in the nineteenth century. Earlier ages treated the critic as a modest judge or servant of the poet. Wilde argues that criticism is itself an independent, creative art.

Mocking Arnold directly, Gilbert declares that the critic's aim is *not* to see the object as in itself it really is, but to see it as it really is *not*—using a poem or painting as raw material to create a new work of beauty. "The highest Criticism," Gilbert says, "is the record of one's own soul."

### The *Preface to The Picture of Dorian Gray* (1891)

When Victorian reviewers attacked *The Picture of Dorian Gray* as immoral, Wilde added a razor-sharp preface of twenty-five epigrams defending artistic freedom:

- "The artist is the creator of beautiful things."
- "There is no such thing as a moral or an immoral book. Books are well written, or badly written. That is all."
- "Vice and virtue are to the artist materials for an art."
- "All art is quite useless."

With that final line, Wilde sealed the break with Victorian moralism: art is not a tool, a sermon or a useful machine—its very uselessness is what makes it free.

### An interesting story

While an undergraduate at Oxford in 1874, the young Oscar Wilde was so inspired by John Ruskin's lectures on the dignity of manual labour that he joined Ruskin's famous "Hinksey Road" dig—rolling a wheelbarrow and swinging a pickaxe alongside other students to repair a muddy country road for local villagers. Yet at the very same university, Wilde discovered Walter Pater's *The Renaissance*, which he later called his "golden book". Wilde's glittering career as a critic was born in the tension between those two Oxford teachers, with Pater's cult of beauty winning out over Ruskin's gospel of toil.

### Key terms

- **Victorian criticism** - literary and cultural criticism of Queen Victoria's reign (1837-1901), divided between moral seriousness (Arnold, Ruskin) and Aestheticism (Pater, Wilde).
- **Disinterestedness** - Arnold's ideal of a critic standing free of political, practical or personal bias to see "the object as in itself it really is".
- **Sweetness and light** - Arnold's phrase (adapted from Swift) in *Culture and Anarchy* for beauty and intelligence.
- **Barbarians, Philistines and Populace** - Arnold's three labels for the Victorian aristocracy, middle class and working class.
- **Hebraism and Hellenism** - Arnold's contrast between strict moral conduct (Hebraism) and open-minded intellectual beauty (Hellenism).
- **Criticism of life** - Arnold's famous definition of poetry in "The Study of Poetry" (1880).
- **High seriousness** - Arnold's standard for supreme poetry (adapted from Aristotle's *spoudaiotes*), combining deep moral truth with the "grand style".
- **Touchstone method** - Arnold's method of testing poems by holding them beside short passages from Homer, Dante, Shakespeare and Milton.
- **Historical and personal estimates** - Arnold's two critical fallacies, contrasted with the **real estimate**.
- **Pathetic fallacy** - Ruskin's term (*Modern Painters*, Vol. 3, 1856) for attributing human emotions to inanimate nature.
- **The Nature of Gothic** - Ruskin's famous chapter in *The Stones of Venice* (1853) praising imperfect, free human craftsmanship over industrial perfection.
- **Aestheticism / Art for art's sake** - the late-Victorian movement (Pater, Wilde) asserting that art exists solely for beauty, independent of morality or utility.
- **Impressionistic criticism** - Pater's approach of asking what personal impression a work of art produces on the critic.
- **Hard, gem-like flame** - Pater's metaphor in the Conclusion to *The Renaissance* for living with intense aesthetic awareness.
- **Condition of music** - Pater's ideal of art in which form and content are completely inseparable.
- **Life imitates Art** - Wilde's witty reversal of Aristotelian imitation in "The Decay of Lying" (1891).
- **The Critic as Artist** - Wilde's 1891 dialogue arguing that criticism is an independent, creative art rather than a secondary service.

## How Victorian criticism shaped what came after

### Ideas that stayed

Victorian criticism left two lasting legacies that still shape how we read today. From Arnold and Ruskin came the conviction that literature matters deeply to culture, society and human dignity—and that critics must hold high standards rather than bow to passing fashion. From Pater and Wilde came the insistence that a work of art must be judged first as art—for its form, style and beauty—rather than reduced to a moral sermon or a history lesson.

### From the Victorians to Modernism

Twentieth-century criticism grew directly out of this Victorian tension. When T. S. Eliot wrote *The Sacred Wood* (1920) and defended "impersonality" and literary tradition, he was arguing both with and against Matthew Arnold. When Formalists and New Critics insisted on reading the poem itself rather than hunting for moral messages, they echoed Pater's fusion of form and content and Wilde's defence of artistic autonomy. Even modern cultural studies and Marxist criticism owe a quiet debt to Arnold's *Culture and Anarchy* and Ruskin's critique of industrial labour.

### How to read the Victorian critics

When you read these four critics, ask three questions of each. What is the relationship between **art and morality**? What is the **critic's job**—a judge, a moral prophet, a sensitive receiver of impressions, or an independent artist? And how does each writer respond to **modern industrial society**? Arnold offers culture; Ruskin offers moral craft; Pater offers intense aesthetic moments; Wilde offers witty liberation. Together, they turn the Victorian era into one of the most dramatic chapters in the history of criticism.

### Important points to remember

- Arnold and Ruskin established the tradition of cultural and social criticism in English.
- Pater and Wilde defended the autonomy of art and the inseparable unity of form and content.
- T. S. Eliot and twentieth-century Modernist critics built directly upon—and argued against—Matthew Arnold.
- Pater's and Wilde's focus on form and aesthetic independence helped prepare the ground for New Criticism.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is Victorian literary criticism?**
It is the literary and cultural criticism written during Queen Victoria's reign (1837-1901). Its four leading figures—Matthew Arnold, John Ruskin, Walter Pater and Oscar Wilde—debated whether art exists to serve moral and social truth or purely for the sake of beauty.

**What did Matthew Arnold mean by the "touchstone method"?**
In "The Study of Poetry" (1880), Arnold advised critics to keep short passages from supreme poets—Homer, Dante, Shakespeare and Milton—in mind and compare other poems against them to judge their real poetic value, avoiding historical or personal bias.

**What is John Ruskin's "pathetic fallacy"?**
Coined in Volume 3 of *Modern Painters* (1856), the pathetic fallacy is the poetic habit of attributing human emotions to inanimate nature—such as calling sea-spray "the cruel, crawling foam"—when strong feeling distorts calm observation.

**Why did Walter Pater say all art aspires towards the condition of music?**
In "The School of Giorgione" (1877), Pater praised music because its form and content are completely fused into one indivisible whole. Great poetry and painting strive to achieve that same seamless unity rather than carrying a detachable moral message.

**What did Oscar Wilde mean by "Life imitates Art"?**
In "The Decay of Lying" (1891), Wilde reversed Aristotle's theory of imitation by arguing that art teaches us how to see and feel. Rather than art passively copying nature, our perception of life and nature is shaped by the artistic images we absorb.

**How did Victorian criticism prepare the way for twentieth-century criticism?**
Arnold's insistence on literary tradition and high standards directly shaped T. S. Eliot, while Pater's and Wilde's defence of artistic autonomy and unified form helped pave the way for Formalism and New Criticism.
