In the autumn of 1066 a Norman duke won a battle on the Sussex coast, and the people who ruled England began to speak a different language. For the next three centuries the royal court used French, the Church and the schools used Latin, and English lived on as the speech of farmers, workers and townspeople. It had no official standing and no standard form. Each region wrote it as it sounded.

Yet this was the age in which English literature found its voice. The language lost most of its old word endings, took in thousands of French words, and became lighter and easier to bend. By 1400 a London civil servant named Geoffrey Chaucer was writing verse that readers still enjoy today. Around him, an unknown poet in the north-west Midlands told how a green giant rode into King Arthur's hall at Christmas. A dreamer in the west Midlands saw the whole of English society gathered in one field. A woman shut in a cell at Norwich wrote down her visions of divine love.

The period closed with a change in technology. In 1476 William Caxton set up a printing press at Westminster, and in 1485 he printed the tales of King Arthur. Books were no longer slow to copy and rare to own. English had come through conquest, plague and rebellion, and it was ready for a new age.

## The world of this age

### Political life

William of Normandy defeated King Harold at Hastings on 14 October 1066 and was crowned in Westminster Abbey on Christmas Day. He gave English land to his Norman followers, and in 1086 his officials carried out a great survey of the kingdom, recorded in **Domesday Book**. The new rulers were French-speaking lords who also held land across the Channel, and that link drew England into long wars with France.

Royal power was tested at home as well. In 1215 the barons forced King John to accept Magna Carta, which limited what a king could demand. The Hundred Years' War began in 1337 and lasted until 1453. English armies won famous victories at Crécy in 1346 and Agincourt in 1415, but the taxes that paid for the war brought anger. In 1381 villagers in Essex refused to pay a poll tax, and thousands of rebels marched on London. The king, Richard II, was only fourteen. He met the Kentish leader Wat Tyler at Smithfield on 15 June, and Tyler was killed there. The promises made to the rebels were broken, but the poll tax was dropped.

The crown itself passed uneasily. In 1399 Richard II was removed by his cousin Henry of Lancaster, who became Henry IV. From 1455 rival branches of the royal family fought the Wars of the Roses. They are usually said to have ended on 22 August 1485, when Henry Tudor defeated and killed Richard III at Bosworth Field. He became Henry VII, and a new dynasty began.

### Social life

English society was built in layers. The king gave land to great lords, the lords gave land to knights, and each held it in return for service in war. At the bottom were the peasants, many of them villeins, who worked their lord's fields, paid dues in labour and goods, and could not leave the manor freely. In the towns, craftsmen and merchants joined guilds that controlled training, prices and quality.

The ideal man of the upper class was the knight: brave, loyal and polite. Stories about knights taught a code called **chivalry** and a fashion called **courtly love**, in which a knight served a noble lady with devotion, often from a distance. Most women worked on the land, in the home or in small trades, though a few, like the merchant's daughter Margery Kempe, travelled far on pilgrimage. The universities at Oxford and Cambridge took shape in this age and trained the clerks and priests who made up most of the educated class.

### Economic life

Wool was England's main wealth. Flocks grazed on the hills, and the wool was sold to the cloth towns of Flanders. The king taxed it at the ports, which is why Chaucer, as controller of customs in London from 1374, spent his working days checking wool, skins and leather. Most people, however, lived by farming, and the manor was the basic unit of country life.

In the summer of 1348 the **Black Death** reached England by sea from France. Estimates of the death toll vary, but it may have killed as many as half of the people. Whole villages were emptied. Workers became scarce, and the survivors could ask for higher wages. Lords and the government tried to hold wages down by law, and that angered the poor. Historians often see this long quarrel as one root of the revolt of 1381. In the longer run, many peasants gained more freedom and better pay.

### Religion

The Catholic Church shaped daily life, and its language was Latin. Parish priests, monks, nuns and friars were part of every community. Pilgrimage was a popular form of devotion, and Canterbury drew crowds to the shrine of Thomas Becket, the archbishop murdered in his cathedral in 1170. The poets of the age knew the Church well and often laughed at its failings, especially greedy friars and sellers of pardons.

Some wanted deeper change. John Wyclif, a teacher at Oxford, argued that ordinary people should be able to read the Bible, and he criticised the wealth of the clergy. He died on the last day of 1384, but his followers, called **Lollards**, kept his ideas alive. An English Bible, translated from the Latin Vulgate, appeared in the early 1380s, and a smoother revision followed about 1388. In the early fifteenth century Church leaders moved against the Lollards and their Bible. Alongside this protest, hermits and anchoresses lived in prayer and wrote guides for others.

### Did you know?

Many English words for farm animals come from Old English, but the words for their meat come from French. Cow and beef, calf and veal, sheep and mutton, swine and pork, deer and venison all follow this pattern. Sir Walter Scott turned the idea into a joke in his novel *Ivanhoe* (1819). The jester Wamba says that a pig keeps its English name while an English servant looks after it, and becomes French "pork" when it reaches the Norman lords' table. Word historians say the real story was slower and messier than the joke, but the pairs are real.

### Important points to remember

- William of Normandy won at Hastings on 14 October 1066 and was crowned on Christmas Day.
- Domesday Book records the great survey of 1086.
- Magna Carta was agreed in 1215.
- The Hundred Years' War ran from 1337 to 1453.
- The Black Death reached England in 1348 and may have killed about half the people.
- The Peasants' Revolt of 1381 began over the poll tax, and Wat Tyler was killed at Smithfield.
- The Wars of the Roses ended at Bosworth in 1485 with Henry Tudor's victory.
- French was the language of court and law, Latin of the Church, and English of the common people.
- John Wyclif's followers, the Lollards, supported an English Bible.

## Literature in this age

### The language

No other period changed English so fast. The old system of word endings, which Old English used to show who did what, wore away. Nouns lost their grammatical gender, and word order became much more fixed, with the subject first, then the verb, then the object, as it is today.

French poured into the vocabulary. Roughly ten thousand French words entered English in these centuries, among them *court*, *justice*, *prison*, *prayer* and *beauty*. Latin words arrived through the Church and the schools. The result was an English with a rich stock of near-synonyms, such as *kingly* (English), *royal* (French) and *regal* (Latin).

There was no standard language. Scholars usually describe five main dialects: Northern, East Midland, West Midland, Southern and Kentish. *Sir Gawain and the Green Knight* was written in a dialect of the north-west Midlands and *Piers Plowman* in the west Midlands. Chaucer wrote in the English of London, a form of East Midland.

The status of English rose slowly. By the late fourteenth century even many nobles were using it, and French was turning into a language learned at school. In 1362 the Pleading in English Act, often called the **Statute of Pleading**, ruled that court cases must be argued in English, though the records were still kept in Latin. Historians debate how much difference it made. In the early fifteenth century, under Henry V, the royal writing office, the Chancery, began to use a form of London English for official papers. This **Chancery Standard**, together with the printing press, helped to fix the written language. Around 1400 the long vowels also began to change in the Great Vowel Shift, which later set Chaucer's sounds apart from our own.

### How the writing survived

Most Middle English writing survives only because someone copied it by hand. The **Auchinleck Manuscript**, made in London between 1330 and 1340, is one of the earliest large collections of English verse. Every text in it is English, and it holds romances such as *Sir Orfeo*. It seems to have been made for well-off readers who were not nobles, which shows that a new reading public was growing.

The poems of the Gawain poet survive in a single book, now called Cotton Nero A.x, made around 1400. If that one manuscript had been lost, *Pearl*, *Cleanness*, *Patience* and *Sir Gawain and the Green Knight* would be unknown. *Piers Plowman* had the opposite fate. More than fifty manuscripts of it survive, a sign of how widely it was read.

Print changed the picture. William Caxton, a London merchant who learned printing abroad, set up the first English press at Westminster in 1476 and printed more than a hundred titles. They included *The Canterbury Tales*, printed within a few years of the press opening, and Malory's *Le Morte d'Arthur*, finished on 31 July 1485.

Luck still mattered. In 1934 two famous finds were made. At Winchester College a teacher, Walter Oakeshott, found a manuscript of Malory that differed from Caxton's printed text. In the same year the only manuscript of *The Book of Margery Kempe* turned up in the home of the Butler-Bowdon family. It lay in a cupboard among old papers and the family's table-tennis equipment.

### Poetry

The first English poems after the Conquest still used old forms. Around 1200 a priest named **Layamon**, who lived at Areley Kings in Worcestershire, wrote the *Brut*, a poem of about 16,000 lines. It tells the legendary history of Britain, and it was the first work in English to tell the story of King Arthur. Layamon took his story from the French poet Wace. Another writer, Orm, produced the *Ormulum*, about 20,000 lines of unrhymed verse on the Gospel readings of the Mass. Its only manuscript is in his own hand.

Poets also tried lighter forms. *The Owl and the Nightingale*, 1,794 lines in rhyming couplets, is a debate between two birds. The nightingale stands for joy and the owl for gloom, and neither wins. Its date is uncertain, but it was probably written around 1200 or a little later. One short song survives with its music: "Sumer is icumen in", a six-part round of about 1260.

The most popular kind of story was the **romance**, a tale of knights, love and marvels. Romances came from France first, and English versions followed. *King Horn* and *Havelok the Dane* are early examples. *Sir Orfeo* retells the Greek story of Orpheus as a fairy tale in which the husband wins his wife back. The Arthurian stories, known as the Matter of Britain, were told again and again.

In the later fourteenth century the old unrhymed, alliterative verse came back in what is called the **alliterative revival**. *Sir Gawain and the Green Knight*, about 2,530 lines in 101 stanzas, is its finest romance. The poet is unknown, and the same hand is credited with *Pearl*, a dream vision in which a grieving speaker meets a young girl who has died, and with *Cleanness* and *Patience*. Each stanza of *Gawain* ends in five short rhymed lines called the **bob and wheel**. The story opens at Camelot at Christmas, when a green giant challenges the court to a beheading game. Gawain cuts off his head, and the giant picks it up and tells Gawain to meet him in a year. On the way Gawain stays in a castle, where the lady tempts him and he hides a green girdle that she says will protect his life. At the Green Chapel the Green Knight swings his axe three times and leaves only a small cut, because Gawain kept the girdle. The knight is the lord of the castle. The poem tests the ideal of the perfect knight and finds that he is human.

William Langland's *Piers Plowman* belongs to the same alliterative tradition but has a different spirit. It is a **dream vision**. A dreamer named Will falls asleep on the Malvern Hills and sees a field full of folk, rich and poor, who stand for the whole of society. In later dreams he sees the seven deadly sins confess and meets an honest ploughman named Piers. He searches for the answer to how a man should live, which the poem calls Do-Well, Do-Better and Do-Best. Langland attacks corrupt clergy and the greed of the powerful. The poem exists in versions called A (1360s), B (late 1370s) and C (1380s). It also holds the earliest certain mention of Robin Hood tales, when the lazy priest Sloth admits that he knows rhymes of Robin Hood better than his prayers.

### Geoffrey Chaucer and his followers

Geoffrey Chaucer was born in London around 1343, the son of a wine merchant. As a young man he served as a page in a noble household, and in 1359 he was captured in France, where the king helped to pay his ransom. He later worked as a soldier, a diplomat who visited Italy, controller of wool customs in London from 1374, and Clerk of the King's Works from 1389. He died in 1400 and was buried in Westminster Abbey, in what later became Poets' Corner.

His poetry is often divided into three phases. In the first he followed French models. *The Book of the Duchess* mourns Blanche, the wife of John of Gaunt, who died in 1368. In the second he learned from Italian writers such as Boccaccio. *The Parliament of Fowls* and *Troilus and Criseyde*, a long love story set in the Trojan War and written in **rhyme royal**, belong here. In the third he turned to English life, and the result was *The Canterbury Tales*. He also translated Boethius and wrote a *Treatise on the Astrolabe* for his young son.

*The Canterbury Tales*, begun about 1387, is a frame story. About thirty pilgrims meet at the Tabard Inn in Southwark on their way to the shrine of Thomas Becket at Canterbury. Their host, Harry Bailly, suggests that each of them should tell tales on the road, and that the best storyteller should win a free supper. Chaucer planned four tales from each pilgrim, two on the way there and two on the way back. He finished only 24, and some of those are incomplete. The General Prologue gives a sharp portrait of each traveller, from the Knight to the Miller, the Wife of Bath and the Pardoner. The tales that follow come in every style: a noble romance, a rude comic story called a **fabliau**, a beast fable, a sermon in prose. Most are in rhyming couplets of ten syllables, and Chaucer is usually credited with bringing both this couplet and the rhyme royal stanza into English poetry.

Chaucer's friend John Gower wrote in French, Latin and English. His English poem *Confessio Amantis*, of about 33,000 lines, tells love stories to teach about the seven deadly sins. In the fifteenth century Thomas Hoccleve and John Lydgate praised Chaucer as their master. In Scotland, Robert Henryson wrote *The Testament of Cresseid*, a sequel to *Troilus*, and William Dunbar wrote the *Lament for the Makaris*, a poem on the death of poets.

### Prose

English prose grew later than English verse and began with religious writing. The *Ancrene Wisse*, from the early thirteenth century, is a guide for women who chose to live shut away in prayer, called **anchoresses**. In the fourteenth century Richard Rolle, Walter Hilton and the unknown author of *The Cloud of Unknowing* wrote guides to prayer for lay readers.

Two women left unusual books. In May 1373, at the age of thirty, Julian of Norwich fell seriously ill and received sixteen visions, which she described in *Revelations of Divine Love*. She wrote a short account first and a longer one years later. Hers is the earliest surviving book in English known to be written by a woman. Margery Kempe, a merchant's daughter from King's Lynn, had fourteen children, then devoted herself to religion, travelled to Jerusalem, Rome and Santiago, and was accused of heresy but cleared. She dictated her life to scribes, and *The Book of Margery Kempe* is the earliest known autobiography in English.

Not all prose was devout. *The Travels of Sir John Mandeville*, written in French about 1356 and soon translated into English, describes a journey through the East, with monsters and marvels. The narrator claims to be an English knight, but the real author is unknown. The *Paston Letters*, written by a Norfolk family in the fifteenth century, show everyday life, lawsuits and the Wars of the Roses from the inside.

The greatest English prose work of the period is *Le Morte d'Arthur* by Sir Thomas Malory. Its author says he finished it in prison about 1469 or 1470, and scholars still argue about which man named Malory he was. He gathered the Arthurian stories from French and English sources into eight long tales. They cover the rise of Arthur, the quest for the Holy Grail, the love of Lancelot and Guinevere, and the fall of the Round Table. Caxton printed the book in 1485 and cut it into twenty-one books and 507 chapters.

### Drama

English drama began in church. Short scenes about Easter and Christmas were acted as part of Latin services, and by the fourteenth century plays in English were being performed outdoors. The most important kind was the **mystery play**, a series of short plays on Bible history from the fall of the angels to the Last Judgment. These cycles were played in summer at the feast of Corpus Christi, on wagons called **pageants**, and each play was usually the work of a craft guild.

Four nearly complete cycles survive, from York, Chester, Wakefield and the group known as N-Town. The York cycle has 48 plays and was played from the mid-fourteenth century until 1569. The Wakefield, or Towneley, cycle has 32 plays, and its finest writer, called the Wakefield Master, is known for wit and satire. His *Second Shepherds' Play* shows a sheep thief, Mak, hiding a stolen lamb in a cradle as if it were a baby, before an angel announces the real birth of Christ.

The other great kind was the **morality play**, an allegory in which the hero stands for all mankind and is pulled between Vices and Virtues. *The Castle of Perseverance*, of about 1425, is the earliest surviving complete example, and *Mankind*, of about 1470, mixes sermon and comedy. The best known is *Everyman*. Death summons an ordinary man, his friend Fellowship refuses to go with him, and only Good Deeds will. It is generally taken to be a version of the Dutch play *Elckerlijc* of about 1495, and it reached print in English in the early sixteenth century.

### An interesting story

In 1490 William Caxton printed a book called *Eneydos* and added a short preface. In it he tells of some merchants sailing down the Thames towards Zeeland. With no wind, they waited, and went ashore at the Foreland to rest. One of them, a mercer named Sheffelde, went into a house and asked for food, and especially for "eggys". The woman of the house said she could speak no French. The merchant grew angry, for he spoke no French either and only wanted eggs. At last another man asked for "eyren", and she understood at once. Caxton ends with a worried question: should a printer write "egges" or "eyren"? One small word had shown him how much English changed from place to place.

### Key terms

- **Middle English** - the English of about 1100 to 1500, with few word endings and many French words.
- **Anglo-Norman** - the form of French spoken in England after the Conquest.
- **Vernacular** - the everyday language of a people, such as English, as against Latin or French.
- **Alliterative revival** - the return of unrhymed alliterative verse in the later fourteenth century, as in *Gawain* and *Piers Plowman*.
- **Bob and wheel** - the five short rhyming lines that close each stanza of *Sir Gawain and the Green Knight*.
- **Romance** - a story of knights, love, adventure and the marvellous.
- **Courtly love** - the code in which a knight serves his lady with devotion and often from a distance.
- **Dream vision** - a poem in which the narrator falls asleep and sees a vision, as in *Piers Plowman* and *Pearl*.
- **Allegory** - a story in which characters and events stand for ideas, as in *Everyman*.
- **Fabliau** - a short, comic and often rude story in verse, such as the Miller's Tale.
- **Rhyme royal** - a seven-line stanza rhyming ababbcc, used by Chaucer in *Troilus and Criseyde*.
- **Heroic couplet** - a pair of rhyming lines of ten syllables, the main form of *The Canterbury Tales*.
- **Breton lay** - a short romance about love and the supernatural, such as *Sir Orfeo*.
- **Mystery play** - a play on Bible history, performed by craft guilds at Corpus Christi.
- **Morality play** - an allegorical play about the soul of mankind, such as *Everyman*.
- **Pageant** - a wagon on which one mystery play was acted.
- **Lollards** - followers of John Wyclif who wanted an English Bible.
- **Anchoress** - a woman who lived shut in a small cell beside a church, as Julian of Norwich did.
- **Chancery Standard** - the London form of English used for official papers from the early fifteenth century.

### Criticism and scholarship

Chaucer's reputation stayed high long after his death. In 1700, in the Preface to his *Fables, Ancient and Modern*, John Dryden called him the father of English poetry, and said of the pilgrims, "here is God's plenty". Dryden also thought Chaucer's lines were rough, because nobody then understood how Middle English had been pronounced.

Proper scholarship began in the nineteenth century. In 1864 F. J. Furnivall founded the Early English Text Society to print early texts that survived only in manuscript, and in 1868 he founded the Chaucer Society. Walter W. Skeat produced a three-text edition of *Piers Plowman* in 1886 and a six-volume *Complete Works of Geoffrey Chaucer* in 1894, with a supplement in 1897. Matthew Arnold, in "The Study of Poetry" (1880), admired Chaucer's "liquid" diction but said he lacked the "high seriousness" of Homer, Dante, Shakespeare and Milton.

In the twentieth century C. S. Lewis's *The Allegory of Love* (1936) studied courtly love and allegory, and D. W. Robertson's *A Preface to Chaucer* (1962) argued that medieval writers should be read in the light of Christian teaching. Carolyn Dinshaw's *Chaucer's Sexual Poetics* (1989) brought questions of gender and desire to Chaucer. The standard edition today is *The Riverside Chaucer* (1987), edited by Larry D. Benson.

The finds of 1934 changed Malory studies. Eugène Vinaver edited the Winchester text in 1947. Later study of ink smudges on its pages showed that it had once been in Caxton's printing shop. For *Piers Plowman*, Lawrence Warner argued in 2011 that the C version began to circulate before the B version, so the usual A, B, C order of the poem's history may be wrong.

### Important points to remember

- Middle English lost most Old English word endings and took in thousands of French words.
- Chaucer wrote in London English, *Gawain* in a north-west Midlands dialect, and *Piers Plowman* in the west Midlands.
- Layamon's *Brut* (about 1200) was the first work in English to tell the story of King Arthur.
- The alliterative revival of the later fourteenth century produced *Gawain*, *Pearl* and *Piers Plowman*.
- *Sir Gawain and the Green Knight* has about 2,530 lines in 101 stanzas, each ending in a bob and wheel.
- *The Canterbury Tales*, begun about 1387, has 24 finished tales told by pilgrims bound for Canterbury.
- Julian of Norwich wrote the earliest surviving English book by a woman, and Margery Kempe the earliest known English autobiography.
- Malory's *Le Morte d'Arthur* was printed by Caxton in 1485.
- The York cycle has 48 plays and the Wakefield cycle has 32.
- *Everyman* is the best-known morality play.

## How the age ends

The date 1500 is a convenience. Nothing in the language or the literature stopped on 1 January of that year. Still, several changes by then marked the end of the medieval pattern. Henry Tudor had been king since 1485, and the long civil wars were over. The printing press, introduced by Caxton in 1476 and carried on after his death by his helper Wynkyn de Worde, was making books cheaper and spreading one London form of English. The Great Vowel Shift was changing the sounds of the language, so that Chaucer's verse began to look rough to later readers.

Medieval forms did not vanish at once. The York plays went on until 1569, and the morality play helped to shape the drama of Shakespeare's time. Chaucer, Langland and Malory kept their readers. But the next age would bring new learning from Italy, the religious quarrel of the Reformation, and a new confidence in English as a language fit for the highest poetry. The writers of these four centuries had prepared the way. They had taken a language of farmers and made it a language of books.

### Important points to remember

- The date 1500 is a convenience, and English literature did not stop on that day.
- Caxton's press, set up in 1476, began to spread one London form of English.
- The Great Vowel Shift, from about 1400, changed English pronunciation.
- Henry VII took the throne in 1485.
- The York plays were performed until 1569.
- Morality plays helped to shape Elizabethan drama.
- The next age brought new learning from Italy and the Reformation.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why is Middle English easier to read than Old English?**
By the thirteenth century most of the old word endings had worn away, and word order had become much more fixed. Many French words had also entered the language. A modern reader can follow Chaucer with a glossary, although spelling and some word forms still differ from ours.

**Why does it matter that Chaucer wrote in London English?**
London was the capital, and its English was a form of East Midland. When the Chancery adopted it for official papers and Caxton printed books in it, it became the base of standard written English. Chaucer's choice therefore stands near the start of the language we use today.

**Who wrote Sir Gawain and the Green Knight?**
Nobody knows. The poem survives in one manuscript, Cotton Nero A.x, together with *Pearl*, *Cleanness* and *Patience*. Because the four poems share a dialect from the north-west Midlands and a similar style, scholars credit them to one unnamed writer, called the Gawain poet or the Pearl poet.

**What is the difference between a mystery play and a morality play?**
A mystery play is one of a cycle of short plays on Bible history, performed by craft guilds on Corpus Christi, from the fall of the angels to the Last Judgment. A morality play is a single allegory in which the hero stands for all mankind and is tempted by Vices and helped by Virtues, as in *Everyman*.

**Why is Piers Plowman hard to date?**
Langland rewrote the poem more than once, so it exists in A, B and C versions, and a later-found Z text. The A version is usually dated to the 1360s, the B to the late 1370s and the C to the 1380s. Scholars still debate the exact order in which the versions circulated.

**Did Middle English literature end exactly in 1500?**
No. The date is a convenience. The age is dated from the Norman Conquest of 1066 to the arrival of the printing press and the first Tudor king. Medieval plays, romances and poems went on being read and performed well into the sixteenth century.
