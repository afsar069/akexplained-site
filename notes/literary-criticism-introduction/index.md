Every reader is already a critic. After a film, friends walk out of the hall and begin to argue. One says the story was weak. Another says the ending was brave. Each gives reasons, and each tries to win the other over. This habit of giving reasons for a response is the seed of literary criticism.

Literary criticism is the same habit, made careful and written down. For more than two thousand years, thinkers have asked what literature is, what it is for, what makes a work good and how it should be read. Their answers did not stay still. The Greeks thought of poetry as imitation of life. Critics of the eighteenth century thought of it as a teacher that gives pleasure. The Romantics thought of it as the voice of a feeling mind. Critics of the twentieth century turned to the words on the page.

This article is the doorway to a series on the history of English literary criticism. It explains the word, the work that critics do, the main kinds of criticism and one useful map of the whole story. The later articles follow that story period by period, from the Greeks to about 1960. Each of them is followed by articles on the main critics of the period.

## What is literary criticism?

### The word and its meaning

The word *critic* comes from the Greek *kritikos*, which meant "able to judge". Behind it stands the verb *krinein*, which meant to separate or to decide. The word reached English through French and Latin in the 1580s. The word *criticism* followed around the year 1600. Judgement, then, has been at the centre of the idea from the beginning.

M. H. Abrams (1912-2015), an American scholar of Romantic literature, gave the description that students most often use today. In *A Glossary of Literary Terms*, he calls literary criticism the overall term for studies that define, classify, analyse, interpret and evaluate works of literature. So criticism is not one single activity. It is a family of activities, and a critic may do some or all of them in one essay.

### Criticism is more than fault-finding

Many people hear the word criticism and think of complaint. English speakers have long done so. John Dryden pushed against this habit in 1677, in the preface to his play *The State of Innocence*. He wrote that finding fault is not the main purpose of criticism. For him, criticism meant a standard of judging well, and its chief part was to notice the excellences that should please a thoughtful reader. He gave the credit for this idea to Aristotle.

Three neighbours of criticism are often confused with it. A *review* is a short report on a new book, play or film. It helps a reader decide whether to read or watch it, and it is written for the moment. *Scholarship* collects facts about a work, such as its date, its sources and its correct text, and these facts support the critic. *Literary theory* asks about the basic ideas behind reading, such as what a text is and how meaning is made. Criticism uses all three, and the lines between them are never sharp.

### Did you know?

Critic, crisis and criterion are cousins. All three grow from the Greek verb *krinein*, to decide. A crisis was first a turning point in an illness, the moment when a decision had to come. A criterion is the standard by which a decision is made. From its early years in English, the word critic has also carried a hint of fault-finding.

### Important points to remember

- The word critic comes from the Greek *kritikos*, "able to judge".
- *Kritikos* comes from the verb *krinein*, to separate or decide.
- Critic entered English in the 1580s, and criticism followed around 1600.
- Abrams describes criticism as defining, classifying, analysing, interpreting and evaluating literature.
- John Dryden said in 1677 that fault-finding is not the main aim of criticism.
- Dryden called criticism a standard of judging well and gave the credit for the idea to Aristotle.
- A review is a short, timely report, while criticism asks deeper questions.

- Critic, crisis and criterion all come from *krinein*.

## What critics do

Abrams's five verbs make a good checklist. They also show how wide the critic's field is.

### Defining and classifying

A critic first asks what kind of thing a work is. Is it a tragedy, a comedy, an epic or a lyric? To answer, the critic needs a definition of the kind and a way of sorting works into groups. Aristotle did this work in the *Poetics*. He defined tragedy and divided it into six parts: plot, character, thought, diction, song and spectacle. Critics have used his sorting ever since, even when they disagreed with him. Classification tells readers what to expect, and it lets critics compare one work with another.

### Analysing

Analysis takes a work apart to see how it is built. A critic may study its images, its structure, its rhythm, its narrator or its point of view. The aim is not to spoil the pleasure of a poem. It is to explain how that pleasure is made. When a critic shows that the short lines of a poem create a feeling of hurry, the poem becomes clearer, not duller.

### Interpreting

Interpretation explains what a work means. Some works are plain, but many are not. A symbol may point two ways, a speaker may not mean what he says, and an ending may stay open. Readers have long argued about why Hamlet delays his revenge, and the argument shows that careful readers can disagree. A good interpretation gives reasons from the text. It also explains why it is better than a rival reading.

### Evaluating

Evaluation asks whether a work is good, and why. This is the hardest of the five tasks, because standards change. Many critics of the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries judged works by rules that they took from the ancient writers. Romantic critics gave more value to feeling and originality. Formalist critics of the twentieth century looked closely at how the parts of a poem work together. A critic who evaluates well makes the standards clear, so that readers can test them.

### How the five tasks work together

In practice the five tasks rarely come apart. A critic who classifies a poem as an elegy has already begun to interpret it, because the label tells the reader what to listen for. A critic who analyses its images is preparing the ground for a judgement of its worth. And every evaluation rests, openly or in secret, on some earlier idea of what literature ought to be. This is why a single essay can seem to do everything at once. When you read criticism for study, it helps to pause and ask which task the writer is performing in each paragraph. That habit makes long essays easier to follow and easier to remember.

### An interesting story

In 405 BCE the Athenian comic poet Aristophanes staged *The Frogs*, and it won first prize at its festival. The hero is Dionysus, the god of theatre. Euripides had died the year before, so Dionysus goes down to the underworld to bring a poet back. There he finds Euripides and Aeschylus arguing over who is the better tragic poet, and he is made the judge. They mock each other's verses, and then the verses are weighed on a pair of scales. Aeschylus wins the weighing, because his lines name heavier things. In the end Dionysus takes Aeschylus back. The play is a joke, but it shows that people judged poetry in public long before anyone wrote a book of criticism.

- Analysis shows how the parts of a work are built and how they work together.

- Evaluation judges the worth of a work, and it is hard because standards change.

- Most good criticism does several of the five tasks at once.

## Kinds of criticism

### Theoretical and practical criticism

Abrams separates two broad kinds. Theoretical criticism sets out a general theory of literature. It offers principles, together with a set of terms and categories, that can be used to identify, analyse and judge works. Aristotle's *Poetics* is the oldest great example in the West. In the twentieth century, I. A. Richards's *Principles of Literary Criticism* (1924) and Northrop Frye's *Anatomy of Criticism* (1957) belong to the same kind.

Practical criticism, which Abrams also calls applied criticism, discusses particular works and writers. Its theory stays in the background. It shapes the reading without being stated. Matthew Arnold's *Essays in Criticism*, which began to appear in 1865, are one example. The close readings of the American New Critic Cleanth Brooks, in *The Well Wrought Urn* (1947), are another. A famous book by I. A. Richards carries the name *Practical Criticism*. It is discussed later in this series, and its title is related to this term but is not the same thing.

### Impressionistic and judicial criticism

Abrams adds that practical criticism is sometimes divided again. Impressionistic criticism tries to put into words the feelings that a work produces in the critic. It values the critic's response above rules. Anatole France described it as the adventures of a sensitive soul among masterpieces. Judicial criticism goes further. It tries to analyse and explain the effects of a work, and to judge it by stated standards. The first kind is warm and personal. The second is careful and open to argument. Most good critics use some of both.

### Prescriptive and descriptive criticism

Prescriptive criticism lays down rules for writers and judges works by them. Descriptive criticism explains what works actually do, without saying that they must do so. Many critics of the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries believed in rules drawn from the ancients, so their criticism often leaned toward the prescriptive. Most modern criticism leans the other way, although few critics are purely one or the other.

### Why the labels overlap

These pairs are tools for thinking, not boxes with locked lids. A single essay can be theoretical in its first pages and practical in its last. A critic who begins with a personal response may go on to explain and defend it, and so move from the impressionistic toward the judicial. Even a critic who claims only to describe may be judging, since choosing which works deserve attention is already a kind of judgement. When you meet a critic in this series, ask which labels fit best, and expect exceptions. The labels help you find your way into a text. They are not meant to replace reading it.

### Four ways of looking at a work

The most useful map of critical history comes from Abrams himself. In *The Mirror and the Lamp: Romantic Theory and the Critical Tradition* (1953), he asks readers to picture the whole situation of a work of art. Four things stand in it: the work itself, the artist who made it, the universe it speaks about and the audience it reaches. Nearly every critical theory, he argues, leans mainly on one of these four.

When a theory looks mainly at the universe, it is called *mimetic*, and it sees literature as an imitation of life. When it looks mainly at the audience, it is called *pragmatic*, and it sees literature as a means to teach and to please. When it looks mainly at the artist, it is called *expressive*, and it sees literature as an outpouring of the writer's mind and feeling. When it looks mainly at the work, it is called *objective*, and it treats the work as a self-contained object to be studied for its own form.

| View | Looks mainly at | Literature is seen as | Strongest, in broad terms |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mimetic | The universe | An imitation of life | The ancient world |
| Pragmatic | The audience | A means to teach and please | The Renaissance and the eighteenth century |
| Expressive | The artist | An expression of feeling | The Romantic age |
| Objective | The work | A self-contained object | Twentieth-century formalism |

The title of the book is itself a picture of this change. The mirror stands for the older view that literature reflects the world. The lamp stands for the Romantic view that the writer's mind gives out its own light. Abrams used the four views as a map, not as boxes. Most critics mix them, and ideas from one age often live on in the next.

- **Critic** - from the Greek *kritikos*, one who is able to judge.
- **Review** - a short, timely report on a new work, written to guide readers.
- **Scholarship** - the gathering of facts about a work, such as its date, sources and text.
- **Literary theory** - the study of basic ideas about literature and reading.
- **Theoretical criticism** - criticism that sets out general principles, terms and categories.
- **Practical criticism** - criticism that discusses particular works and writers; also called applied criticism.
- **Impressionistic criticism** - criticism that puts into words the feelings a work arouses.
- **Judicial criticism** - criticism that explains the effects of a work and judges it by standards.
- **Prescriptive criticism** - criticism that lays down rules for writers.
- **Descriptive criticism** - criticism that explains what works do, without laying down rules.
- **Mimetic theory** - a theory that sees literature as imitation of the universe.
- **Pragmatic theory** - a theory that judges literature by its effect on the audience.
- **Expressive theory** - a theory that sees literature as the expression of the artist's feeling.
- **Objective theory** - a theory that studies the work as a self-contained object.
- **Mimesis** - the Greek word for imitation, central to ancient views of art.
- **Catharsis** - Aristotle's word for the purifying of pity and fear that tragedy brings about.
- **Canon** - the group of works that a culture treats as most worth reading.
- **Close reading** - careful attention to the words and form of a short text, as in the New Criticism.

## A map of the whole history

The later articles in this series follow time. Here is the route in short.

### The classical foundations

Plato (c. 428-348 BCE) began the argument. He doubted that imitative poetry could be trusted, and in the *Republic* he would not welcome imitative poets in his ideal city. He argued that a poem copies the things we see, and that those things are themselves only copies of a higher truth, so poetry stands far from reality. His pupil Aristotle (384-322 BCE) answered in the *Poetics*. He held that imitation comes naturally to human beings and that a well-made tragedy can teach and move its audience. He described tragedy as the imitation of a serious action that arouses pity and fear and so brings about a purifying of such feelings, which he called *catharsis*. Critics still argue about exactly what he meant by that word. The Roman poet Horace (65-8 BCE) said in his *Ars Poetica* that poets wish to benefit, to delight or to do both. *On the Sublime* is a Greek treatise on the lofty power of great writing. Its author is unknown, but it is traditionally given the name Longinus, and it is usually dated to about the first century CE.

### Renaissance criticism (1500-1660)

The English story begins in the sixteenth century with arguments about whether poetry is useful or harmful. Stephen Gosson attacked poetry and the stage in *The School of Abuse* (1579). Sir Philip Sidney answered in *The Defence of Poesy*, which was printed in 1595, after his death. He argued that poetry teaches and pleases, and that the poet can move readers toward virtue better than the philosopher or the historian. George Puttenham's *The Art of English Poesy* (1589) and the notes of Ben Jonson belong to the same lively age.

### Neoclassical criticism (1660-1780)

After 1660, many English critics admired order, reason and the lessons of the ancients. John Dryden wrote *An Essay of Dramatic Poesy* (1668) as a conversation between four speakers. Samuel Johnson later called him the father of English criticism. Alexander Pope gave advice to critics in verse, in *An Essay on Criticism* (1711). Joseph Addison wrote essays on *Paradise Lost* for *The Spectator*. Johnson's *Preface to Shakespeare* (1765) and his *Lives of the Poets* (1779-81) mark the high point of the age.

### Romantic criticism (1780-1837)

In the Romantic age, the centre moved from the audience to the artist. William Wordsworth's Preface to *Lyrical Ballads* (1800) argued that poetry should use the language of ordinary people and should grow out of strong feeling. Samuel Taylor Coleridge's *Biographia Literaria* (1817) gave a new account of the poet's imagination. Percy Bysshe Shelley wrote *A Defence of Poetry* in 1821, but it was published only in 1840. William Hazlitt and John Keats, in his letters, added their own sharp and personal voices.

### Victorian criticism (1837-1901)

Victorian critics asked what literature could do for a changing society. Matthew Arnold wrote "The Function of Criticism at the Present Time" (1864) and *Essays in Criticism* (1865). John Ruskin and Thomas Carlyle judged art and writing by their moral and social worth. Walter Pater's *The Renaissance* (1873) turned attention to the intensity of aesthetic experience. Oscar Wilde carried that view further in *Intentions* (1891).

### Early twentieth century (1900-1930)

A. C. Bradley's *Shakespearean Tragedy* (1904) studied Shakespeare's heroes as if they were living characters. T. S. Eliot's essay "Tradition and the Individual Talent" (1919) argued that a poet must work within a tradition and that poetry is an escape from personality. I. A. Richards's *Principles of Literary Criticism* (1924) studied how readers respond to poems, and his later work made close reading a classroom habit.

### Mid twentieth century (1930-1960)

William Empson's *Seven Types of Ambiguity* (1930) showed how many meanings a few lines can hold. F. R. Leavis and the journal *Scrutiny* (1932-53) argued for moral seriousness in reading, and Leavis's *The Great Tradition* (1948) set out his view of the English novel. In the United States, the New Critics, among them John Crowe Ransom, Cleanth Brooks and W. K. Wimsatt, treated the poem as a self-contained object. The name comes from Ransom's book *The New Criticism* (1941). Northrop Frye's *Anatomy of Criticism* (1957) tried to give criticism a system of its own. After about 1960, a new wave of theory arrived, and it is covered in a separate series.

### How the periods connect

Read together, the periods show Abrams's four views taking turns. The ancient writers argued mostly about the relation of poetry to the world and to its audience. The critics of the Renaissance and of the age of Dryden and Johnson kept the audience in view and asked what poetry should teach and how it should please. The Romantics turned toward the writer and asked where poems come from. The critics of the twentieth century turned toward the poem and asked how it is made. Each turn was also a reply to the one before it, and that is why the history reads best as a conversation across time.

### Important points to remember

- Plato doubted imitative poetry, and Aristotle answered him in the *Poetics*.
- Horace said poets wish to benefit, to delight or to do both.
- *On the Sublime* is traditionally ascribed to Longinus, but its author is unknown.
- Sidney's *Defence of Poesy* was printed in 1595 and answered attacks such as Gosson's *The School of Abuse* (1579).
- Dryden's *An Essay of Dramatic Poesy* appeared in 1668, and Johnson called him the father of English criticism.
- Pope's *An Essay on Criticism* (1711) is a poem of advice to critics, and Johnson's *Preface to Shakespeare* appeared in 1765.
- Wordsworth's Preface to *Lyrical Ballads* appeared in 1800, and Coleridge's *Biographia Literaria* in 1817.
- Shelley's *A Defence of Poetry* was written in 1821 and published in 1840.
- Arnold's "The Function of Criticism at the Present Time" appeared in 1864.
- The name New Criticism comes from John Crowe Ransom's book of 1941.

## Why criticism matters

### What critics have changed

Critics do more than comment. They sometimes change how writers write and how readers read. Wordsworth's Preface worked like a statement of purpose for a new kind of poetry. Johnson's *Preface to Shakespeare* defended Shakespeare against critics who wanted strict rules, and it argued that his plays were true to human nature. Eliot's essays in the 1920s changed the way many readers saw seventeenth-century poets such as John Donne.

Critics also help to build the canon. A canon is the group of works that a culture treats as most worth reading. No one hands down such a list. Critics, teachers and readers make it through argument, and they keep changing it. When a lost writer returns to the reading lists, or a famous writer fades, a critic has usually been at work.

### What criticism gives the reader

For an ordinary reader, criticism does three useful things. It gives words to a response that was only a feeling. A reader who enjoyed a poem can learn, from a critic, to say why. It also shows that one work can be read in more than one way, so that the reader meets other minds as well as the text. And it works as a check on the reader's own habits. Everyone brings tastes and blind spots to a book, and a good critic can make those visible. This is why the older essays in this series are still worth reading. Their answers may be out of date, but their questions are not.

### Critics who were also writers

Many of the critics in this series were also poets, and that is no accident. Sidney wrote sonnets as well as his *Defence*. Dryden and Pope were among the great poets of their time. Wordsworth wrote his Preface to explain his own poems, and Coleridge and Shelley wrote their best-known criticism as poets thinking about the art they practised. Arnold was a poet before he made his name as a critic, and Eliot was a poet whose essays helped to shape the taste of his own time. A poet who writes criticism usually knows from the inside how a poem is made, and this gives such criticism a special force. Other critics, such as F. R. Leavis, worked mainly as university teachers and editors, and they spoke as readers and guides. Both kinds of critic appear in this series.

### How to read this series

Keep Abrams's four questions in mind. For each critic, ask whether the writer is looking mainly at the world, the audience, the artist or the work. The answer usually explains most of the essay. Then ask what the critic wanted to defend or change. Almost every great critical text began as an argument against something. Sidney argued against attacks on poetry, Wordsworth against stiff poetic habits, Arnold against narrow habits of thought and Richards against careless reading.

The series has two layers. The first layer is a set of eight period articles: this introduction, then classical, Renaissance, neoclassical, Romantic, Victorian, early twentieth century and mid twentieth century. The second layer is a set of articles on the main critics of each period. The dates of the periods are approximate, because critical ideas do not change on a fixed day.

### Important points to remember

- Critics sometimes change how writers write and how readers read.
- Wordsworth's Preface argued for the language of ordinary people and for poetry that grows out of strong feeling.
- Johnson's *Preface to Shakespeare* defended Shakespeare against strict rules.
- Eliot's essays changed how many readers saw poets such as Donne.
- A canon is the group of works that a culture treats as most worth reading.
- Criticism gives readers words for their responses, shows other ways of reading and checks their blind spots.
- Many critics in this series, among them Sidney, Dryden, Pope, Wordsworth, Coleridge, Shelley, Arnold and Eliot, were also poets.
- Asking which of Abrams's four things a critic looks at is a good way into any critic.
- The series has eight period articles, followed by articles on the main critics.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is literary criticism?**
Literary criticism is the study of literary works. Following Abrams, it defines, classifies, analyses, interprets and evaluates them, and a critic may do some or all of these tasks in one piece of writing.

**Is criticism the same as a book review?**
No. A review is a short, timely report that helps readers decide whether to read a new work. Criticism asks deeper questions about meaning, form and value, and it aims to say something that lasts.

**What did M. H. Abrams add to the study of criticism?**
In *The Mirror and the Lamp* (1953), Abrams showed that nearly every critical theory leans mainly on one of four things: the universe, the audience, the artist or the work. He called these views mimetic, pragmatic, expressive and objective.

**What is the difference between theoretical and practical criticism?**
Theoretical criticism sets out general principles and terms for studying literature. Practical criticism, also called applied criticism, discusses particular works and writers, with its theory kept in the background.

**Why does this series stop at about 1960?**
After about 1960, a new wave of theory arrived, and it is covered in a separate series. This series follows the older line of criticism, from the Greeks to the New Critics and Northrop Frye.

**Which critics does this series cover?**
It covers the main critics of each period, from Plato, Aristotle, Horace and Longinus to Sidney, Dryden, Pope, Johnson, Wordsworth, Coleridge, Shelley, Arnold, Pater, Eliot, Richards, Leavis and Frye. Each period article is followed by articles on its main critics.
