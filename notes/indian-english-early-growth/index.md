In 1876 a book of French poems turned into English verse came out with one name on its title page, Toru Dutt. Edmund Gosse reviewed it in *The Examiner*. K. R. Srinivasa Iyengar reports that Gosse declared that, if modern French literature were lost, a great number of its poems might be rebuilt from this Indian version. Iyengar adds that French and English were alike foreign languages to the young translator.

This article covers the years from 1857 to 1920. The first date is the year of the revolt against Company rule and of the first Indian universities. The second is a working boundary, like the dates in the last article. The next article begins with the 1920s, when the independence movement under Mohandas K. Gandhi became a mass movement.

The article first describes the world of these years, and then the writing that grew out of it. Every hard term is explained where it first appears.

## The world of this period

### Political life

The revolt of 1857, which British writers called the Mutiny, ended the rule of the East India Company. Queen Victoria's proclamation of 1 November 1858 declared that India would from then on be governed by the Crown. This article calls the new system **Crown rule**. The Crown's chief officer in India was the **Viceroy**.

Indians soon found a public voice of their own. In December 1885 the **Indian National Congress** met at Bombay. Allan Octavian Hume, a retired civil servant, was its general secretary, and W. C. Bonnerjee, a Calcutta lawyer, was elected its president. Seventy-two delegates attended. The Congress began as a loyal and moderate association, and it was not at first a political party. Its demand for independence grew into the mass movement led by Gandhi in the 1920s and 1930s.

In 1905 the Viceroy, Lord Curzon, divided Bengal into two parts, and this became known as the **Partition of Bengal**. Indians answered with the **swadeshi** movement, a plan to boycott British goods and to use Indian-made goods instead. In 1911 the two parts of Bengal were reunited, and the capital of British India moved to Delhi.

### Social life

Iyengar describes Bengal in the later nineteenth century as seething with literary activity of all kinds. The great Bengali novelist Bankimchandra Chatterji was then producing his novels in quick succession. He began one novel in English but did not finish it, and he wrote most of his work in his mother tongue. Iyengar says that his presence on the scene inspired other writers, and that the last quarter of the century saw a great increase in English and **vernacular** publications. A vernacular is a local language of the country, such as Bengali or Gujarati. Iyengar writes that all India seemed to have woken from a long stupor. The movement of reform and new writing in these years is known as the **Indian renaissance**.

Women of educated families took a growing share in this world. Aru and Toru Dutt learned French and English abroad, and Toru wrote a French novel as well as English verse. Their father, Gobind Chandra Dutt, also published English verse, and Iyengar calls the family a cultured one.

Reform remained a public cause. Behramji Malabari wrote against the marriage of very young girls and worked to raise the "age of consent". Iyengar says his work bore fruit in the raising of the age of consent and in public attention to the plight of the Indian widow.

### Economic life

Few writers of the time lived by writing alone. Most earned a living in government service, law, teaching or journalism. Romesh Chunder Dutt passed the Indian Civil Service examination in England and rose to be a divisional commissioner in Bengal. Nagesh Wishwanath Pai was first a pleader in the Bombay High Court and then a government reader at Sholapur. Manmohan Ghose taught English literature at the Presidency College in Calcutta. Sri Aurobindo was for a time a professor at Baroda. Malabari had to earn his own bread from about the age of twelve.

Many books were printed in London. Toru Dutt's **ballads**, which are stories told in verse, came out in 1882 from the London firm of Kegan Paul. Sarojini Naidu's *The Bird of Time* came out in 1912 from William Heinemann of London and the John Lane Company of New York. Romesh Dutt's verse versions of the *Mahabharata* and the *Ramayana*, the two great **epics** of India, won a place in Dent's Everyman's Library, Iyengar notes. Dutt also wrote books on the economic condition of India in the nineteenth century.

### Schools, colleges and the press

Study abroad shaped several poets. Toru Dutt attended a French school at Nice from 1869, and in 1871 the sisters went to the Higher Lectures for Women at Cambridge. Sri Aurobindo was sent to England as a boy and entered the University of Cambridge. Sarojini Naidu went to London and Cambridge on a state scholarship from the Nizam of Hyderabad. Manmohan Ghose also studied at Oxford.

Journals gave new writers their first readers. Toru Dutt's English novel appeared in *The Bengal Magazine*. B. R. Rajam Iyer's novel *Vasudeva Sastri* came out in *Prabuddha Bharata* in the closing years of the nineteenth century. Sri Aurobindo's long essays appeared in the philosophical review *Arya* between 1914 and 1921.

### Religion

Iyengar holds that Rammohan Roy's work awakened Hindu society. He says that this awakening bore fruit in reforms such as the end of sati and the remarriage of widows. It also produced leaders such as Ramakrishna and Vivekananda, Dayanand Saraswati, Sri Aurobindo and Gandhi. Rabindranath Tagore was born into a family that took part in the Brahmo Samaj, the society founded by Rammohan Roy. Swami Vivekananda is described in the section on prose.

### Did you know?

Behramji Malabari became one of the best-known Indian writers of his day. Iyengar says that he passed the matriculation examination only at the fourth attempt. He had been earning his own living since he was about twelve years old.

## Literature in this period

### The language and the readers

Iyengar uses the word **Indo-Anglian** for an Indian who writes in English. The Indo-Anglians of these years were a small group. Several had studied in England or France, and some wrote in more than one language. Toru Dutt wrote in English and French, and Rabindranath Tagore wrote mainly in Bengali. Their books were often printed in London, as the first part of this article showed.

The poets borrowed forms from English verse, and their subjects were often Indian. Toru Dutt retold the legends of her own country, and Sarojini Naidu wrote about its fishermen and weavers. Novelists in English were few, and Iyengar says that very few Indians tried English fiction before 1900.

### Toru Dutt

Toru Dutt was born on 4 March 1856 and died on 30 August 1877, aged twenty-one. She came back to Bengal at the end of 1873, after her years at Nice and Cambridge. Her sister Aru, with whom she had studied, died on 23 July 1874, aged twenty.

Her first book was *A Sheaf Gleaned in French Fields*, published in 1876. It holds English verse translations of French poems, mostly by Romantic poets. Only Toru's name stood on the title page. For that reason Gosse, in his review in *The Examiner*, credited her with Aru's pieces as well.

The book that keeps her name alive is *Ancient Ballads and Legends of Hindustan*, which appeared in 1882, five years after her death. The London firm of Kegan Paul published it, with an introductory memoir by Gosse. It contains nine ballads and seven occasional poems. The ballads retell Indian legends, among them the stories of Savitri, Dhruva, Prahlad and Ekalavya, and the story of Dasaratha and Sindhu. Some pieces use the **octosyllabic line**, a line of eight syllables. Others use **blank verse**, which is unrhymed verse in lines of ten syllables.

The best-known of the occasional poems is *Our Casuarina Tree*, which is part of the same volume. It is a poem of memory. The poet recalls her brother Abju, who died in 1865, and she compares the murmur of the tree to the sound of the sea on a shingle beach.

Toru Dutt also wrote novels. Her French novel, *Le Journal de Mademoiselle d'Arvers*, was published in Paris in 1879. Her English novel, *Bianca, or The Young Spanish Maiden*, appeared in *The Bengal Magazine* from January to April 1878. Both were printed after her death.

### Epic and narrative verse

Romesh Chunder Dutt (1848-1909) came to literature from public life. He retired from the civil service before he was fifty. Iyengar adds that he sat in the Legislative Council and served the state of Baroda as revenue minister and as **dewan**, the chief minister of the state.

Dutt published *Lays of Ancient India* in 1894. His best-known verse work is a pair of retellings of the two great epics. The *Mahabharata* version appeared in 1898, and Iyengar dates the *Ramayana* version to 1900. Both are written in **couplets**, which are pairs of rhymed lines, in the **Locksley Hall metre**, with prose passages that join the parts of the story together.

Nabokissen Ghose (1837-1918) wrote under the name Ram Sharma. Debendrachandra Mullick collected his verse. The titles include *The Last Day*, *Shiva Ratri*, *Bhagabati Gita* and *Daksha Yagna*.

Nagesh Wishwanath Pai, who was born about 1860, wrote *The Angel of Misfortune* (1904). It is a **metrical romance**, which is a long story of adventure told in verse. It tells of King Vikrama in ten books and more than 5,000 lines of blank verse. Pai died about 1920.

### Manmohan Ghose

Manmohan Ghose was the elder brother of Sri Aurobindo. He went to England early in his life, and he studied at Oxford. In 1890 his poems appeared in *Primavera*, a book of verse by four young poets. The other three were Cripps, Binyon and Stephen Phillips.

He later taught English literature at the Presidency College in Calcutta. His books of verse include *Love Songs and Elegies*. His wife died in 1918, and in his last years he lost his sight. He died in 1924.

### Sri Aurobindo

Sri Aurobindo was born at Calcutta on 15 August 1872 and died at Pondicherry on 5 December 1950. In 1908 he was imprisoned, and afterwards he settled at Pondicherry. His English verse includes the narrative poems *Urvasie*, *Love and Death*, *Baji Prabhou* and *Vidula*. A narrative poem is a poem that tells a story. He also wrote the play *Perseus the Deliverer*, and he translated Kalidasa's *Vikramorvasie* and C. R. Das's *Sagar-Sangit*.

His prose belongs to the review *Arya*, where his long essays appeared from 1914 to 1921. Several of his best-known books grew out of those pages. *The Life Divine* appeared as a book in 1939. *Essays on the Gita* came out in 1922. The other works include *The Future Poetry*, *The Synthesis of Yoga* and *The Secret of the Veda*.

### Sarojini Naidu

Sarojini Naidu was born at Hyderabad on 13 February 1879 and died at Lucknow on 2 March 1949. Her gifts showed early, for she passed the Madras matriculation at the age of twelve. She married Dr. Naidu in spite of caste prejudice, as Iyengar notes.

She published *The Golden Threshold* in 1905, *The Bird of Time* in 1912 and *The Broken Wing* in 1917. *The Bird of Time* carries an introduction by Gosse, and it contains the poem *The Bangle Sellers*. Iyengar names a group of her short poems: *Coromandel Fishers*, *Indian Weavers*, *Palanquin-Bearers*, *To a Buddha Seated on a Lotus* and *The Flute-Player of Brindaban*.

Most of these are **lyrics**, which are short poems that express a feeling or picture a scene. Many take their subjects from working people, such as fishermen, weavers, bangle-sellers and palanquin-bearers. She is known as the Nightingale of India.

### Rabindranath Tagore

Rabindranath Tagore was born in 1861 and died on 7 August 1941. His grandfather Dwarkanath was a friend of Rammohan Roy, and his father Debendranath, called the Maharshi, was a pillar of the Brahmo Samaj. Iyengar says that Tagore was prominent in the agitation against the Partition of Bengal, though he did not follow its more extreme turns.

Tagore wrote mainly in Bengali, but he also made English versions of his own work. *Gitanjali* appeared in Bengali in 1910. The English *Gitanjali* followed in 1912, with an introduction by W. B. Yeats. In 1913 Tagore received the Nobel Prize in Literature. The citation praised his "profoundly sensitive, fresh and beautiful verse". He also translated *The Gardener* and *The Crescent Moon* himself.

He was knighted in 1915. After the Jallianwala Bagh massacre of 1919 he gave up the knighthood.

*The Home and the World* appeared in Bengali as *Ghare Baire* in 1916, and in English in 1919. The novel is set in the swadeshi years, and three people tell it in turn: Nikhil, Bimala and Sandip. Two more of his novels, *Gora* and *The Wreck*, are also known to English readers. His plays reached English readers in versions such as *Chitra*, *Sacrifice*, *King and Queen* and *The Post Office*.

### Fiction

Iyengar notes that very few Indians attempted English fiction before 1900. English readers met Bankimchandra Chatterji through translation. Iyengar lists English versions of his novels, among them *The Poison Tree* (1884), *Kapalkundala* (1885), *Durgeshnandini* (1890), *Krishnakanta's Will* (1895) and *The Two Rings* (1897).

Among Indians who wrote novels in English, B. R. Rajam Iyer wrote *Vasudeva Sastri*, which appeared in *Prabuddha Bharata* and was republished in *Rambles in Vedanta* in 1905. He died at the age of twenty-six. A. Madhaviah wrote *Thillai Govindan* and *Panju*. T. Ramakrishna wrote *Padmini* (1903) and *The Dive for Death* (1912).

**Purdah** is the custom of keeping women out of the sight of men who are not close relatives. Cornelia Sorabji's books include *Love and Life behind the Purdah* (1901), *Sun-Babies* (1904) and *Between the Twilights* (1908). Jogendra Singh wrote *Nur Jahan* (1909) and *Nasrin* (1915). S. K. Ghosh published *The Prince of Destiny* (1909), and S. M. Mitra published *Hindupore* (1909). Romesh Chunder Dutt's novels *The Lake of Palms* (1902) and *The Slave Girl of Agra* (1909) also appeared in English.

### Prose and journalism

Behramji Malabari (1853-1912) was born at Surat. He studied at the Surat Mission School under the Rev. Nixon, and he earned his living from about the age of twelve. His English verse was published as *The Indian Muse in English Garb* in 1876. His prose books include *Gujarat and Gujaratis* (1882) and *The Indian Eye on English Life* (1893). A life of Malabari by Gidumal carries an introduction by Florence Nightingale.

Nagesh Wishwanath Pai published *Stray Sketches in Chakmakpore* in 1894. It is a book in prose.

Swami Vivekananda, Iyengar says, was the first to put Indian philosophy on the world map. He lectured on Karma Yoga, Bhakti Yoga, Raja Yoga and Jnana Yoga. Iyengar adds that his active career lasted only about ten years.

### An interesting story

Sarojini Naidu began as a student in England who wrote in the manner of English verse. Edmund Gosse read her early poems and gave her plain advice. She should stop writing about robins and skylarks, he said, and write about her own country. She took the advice. She destroyed her imitative verse and started again, and she turned to the fishermen, weavers and bangle-sellers of India. Her first book of poems, *The Golden Threshold*, appeared in 1905. Gosse later wrote the introduction to her book *The Bird of Time*, which was published in 1912.

### Key terms

- **Crown rule** - the direct government of India by the British Crown after 1858.
- **Viceroy** - the chief officer of the Crown in India.
- **Indian National Congress** - the association founded at Bombay in 1885 that grew into the main movement for independence.
- **Partition of Bengal** - Lord Curzon's division of Bengal into two parts in 1905.
- **Swadeshi** - the movement to boycott British goods and use Indian-made goods.
- **Vernacular** - a local language of the country, such as Bengali or Gujarati.
- **Indian renaissance** - the movement of reform and new writing in nineteenth-century India.
- **Ballad** - a story told in verse.
- **Epic** - a long poem about heroes and great events.
- **Indo-Anglian** - Iyengar's word for an Indian who writes in English.
- **Octosyllabic line** - a line of verse with eight syllables.
- **Blank verse** - unrhymed verse in lines of ten syllables.
- **Dewan** - the chief minister of a state.
- **Couplet** - a pair of rhymed lines.
- **Locksley Hall metre** - the long rhymed couplet of Tennyson's poem *Locksley Hall*.
- **Metrical romance** - a long story of adventure told in verse.
- **Lyric** - a short poem that expresses a feeling or pictures a scene.
- **Purdah** - the custom of keeping women out of the sight of men who are not close relatives.

### Criticism and scholarship

The main survey behind these notes is K. R. Srinivasa Iyengar's *The Indian Contribution to English Literature* (1945). Iyengar judges the writers one by one, and his verdicts shape much of what follows.

Edmund Gosse, an English critic, stood behind several of these poets. He reviewed *A Sheaf Gleaned in French Fields* in *The Examiner*, wrote the memoir for the 1882 edition of Toru Dutt's ballads, and introduced *The Bird of Time*. Iyengar judges that Toru Dutt handled the octosyllabic line well and finds her blank verse rather stiff.

George Sampson, in the *Concise Cambridge History of English Literature*, called Manmohan Ghose the most remarkable Indian poet in English. Iyengar takes a cooler view of Ram Sharma. He records a claim that Ram Sharma was perhaps the greatest Indian poet in English verse, and he thinks the claim overstated.

Iyengar calls Sri Aurobindo the most outstanding Indo-Anglian and notes that Aurobindo wrote only in English. He also quotes Aurobindo on Bankim's "religion of patriotism". On Tagore, Iyengar treats the Nobel award of 1913 as a landmark for Indian writing in English. He also warns against overrating it.

- Romesh Chunder Dutt retold the *Mahabharata* in couplets and published it in 1898.

## How the period ends

The dates in this article are working boundaries, and the writers did not stop in 1920. In the 1920s the independence movement under Gandhi became a mass movement, and the next article begins there.

Several lines of this story close near the boundary. Sri Aurobindo's review *Arya* ended in 1921, and *Essays on the Gita* came out in 1922. Visva-Bharati at Shantiniketan became a university in 1921. Manmohan Ghose died in 1924. In 1925 Sarojini Naidu became the first Indian woman to preside over the Indian National Congress.

Other careers ran on. Tagore died in 1941, and Sarojini Naidu died in 1949. Sri Aurobindo's *Collected Poems and Plays* appeared in 1942. His long poem *Savitri: A Legend and a Symbol* was published in 1950, the year of his death. Toru Dutt had told the story of Savitri in a ballad long before, in the book of 1882.

The next article takes up the novelists and prose writers of the freedom struggle, such as Mulk Raj Anand, R. K. Narayan, Raja Rao, Gandhi and Nehru.

## Frequently asked questions

**Which years does this article cover, and why?**
It covers 1857 to 1920. The first date is the year of the revolt against Company rule and of the first Indian universities. The second is a working boundary, because the next article begins with the 1920s, when the independence movement under Gandhi became a mass movement.

**Who was Toru Dutt?**
Toru Dutt (1856-1877) was a poet and translator who died at the age of twenty-one. She published *A Sheaf Gleaned in French Fields* in 1876, and her *Ancient Ballads and Legends of Hindustan* appeared in 1882, after her death.

**What does Indo-Anglian mean?**
Iyengar uses the word Indo-Anglian for an Indian who writes in English. He calls Sri Aurobindo the most outstanding Indo-Anglian.

**Why is Tagore in an article on Indian writing in English?**
Tagore wrote mainly in Bengali, but he also made English versions of his own work. The English *Gitanjali* appeared in 1912, and he received the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1913.

**What is Sarojini Naidu remembered for?**
She is remembered for her lyrics, among them *The Bangle Sellers* and *Coromandel Fishers*, and for the books *The Golden Threshold* (1905), *The Bird of Time* (1912) and *The Broken Wing* (1917). In 1925 she became the first Indian woman to preside over the Indian National Congress.

**Which legend was told by both Toru Dutt and Sri Aurobindo?**
Both told the story of Savitri. Toru Dutt's ballad on Savitri belongs to her book of 1882, and Sri Aurobindo's *Savitri: A Legend and a Symbol* was published in 1950.
