Imagine a short story about a girl in a small town in Uttar Pradesh. She wakes before everyone else to make tea and pack her brother's tiffin before he leaves for coaching classes. She studies late at night, after the last plate has been washed. When the examination results arrive, her name stands at the top of the district list.

Give this story to four readers. The first admires the girl and asks why she never complains. The second asks who does the housework and who gets the money for coaching. The third notices an old pattern, in which an overlooked child passes a hard test and rises. The fourth listens for what the girl never says, such as jealousy of her brother.

All four have read the same words, but their questions differ. Behind each question lie ideas about what a story is, where its meaning comes from and what deserves attention. **Literary theory** studies ideas of this kind. Our earlier series followed the history of English literary criticism, and this new series turns to literary theory and cultural studies. It begins by asking what theory is, how it rose and why some scholars have opposed it.

## What literary theory means

### A working definition

Vince Brewton, writing in the *Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy*, offers a clear place to begin:

> "Literary theory" is the body of ideas and methods we use in the practical reading of literature.

For Brewton, theory does not state the meaning of a particular work. It is an account of the principles, or tools, with which readers try to understand literature. He also holds that all literary interpretation rests on some basis in theory.

A simple case shows why. One student praises a novel because every part fits into a single design, while another praises it for showing the lives of poor workers honestly. The first trusts that unity marks good art, and the second trusts that fiction should tell the truth about society. Theory brings such beliefs into the open. It asks where meaning comes from, what makes writing literature and how far class, race and gender matter.

### Where the word comes from

Pramod K. Nayar traces the word "theory" to the Greek ***theoria***, whose senses include contemplation, speculation and looking. From this history he draws a simple lesson: theory is a way of seeing, of looking very closely at texts. A theory works rather like a lens. A reader who follows images and rhythm may not ask who owns the land in a story. A reader who follows money and land may hurry past a fine metaphor.

### Literary criticism and literary theory

Theory is easier to grasp when it is placed beside criticism. M. H. Abrams calls **literary criticism** the overall term for studies that define, classify, analyse, interpret and evaluate works of literature. **Theoretical criticism** sets out general principles, with terms for studying works and standards for judging them. **Applied criticism**, also called **practical criticism**, deals with particular works and writers, and its principles are often left unspoken.

M. A. R. Habib says that the practice of criticism is applied to texts, while theory examines the principles behind that practice. For him, theory reveals the motives behind a way of reading and its links with ideology, power and the unconscious. Above all, it shows that such a practice is not natural but has a history.

| Point of difference | Applied criticism | Literary theory |
|---|---|---|
| Main concern | A particular work or writer | The principles behind reading and judging |
| A sample question | Why does Hamlet delay his revenge? | What do we assume when we treat Hamlet as a real person with a mind? |
| Place of principles | Often left unspoken | Stated openly and examined |

The two are partners. A critic who explains Hamlet's delay is already using ideas about character, and a theorist of character needs real plays to test an argument. Abrams calls Aristotle's *Poetics*, from the fourth century BC, the earliest treatise of theoretical criticism and one of lasting importance. Its argument keeps Greek plays and poems in view.

### A newer and wider meaning of theory

The word "theory" has also gained a special sense, which Abrams explains in his account of **poststructuralism**. This broad set of approaches took the lead from structuralism in the 1970s. Here "theory" standing alone often meant a general account of how signs produce meaning in all human activity. It usually reached beyond language to the sign systems of the mind and of society.

Jonathan Culler, in *Literary Theory: A Very Short Introduction* (1997), picks out four features. Theory is **interdisciplinary**, because its ideas have effects beyond the field where they began. It is analytical and speculative, working out what is involved in matters such as language, meaning or sex. It also criticises common sense, and it is **reflexive**, which means that it thinks about thinking.

These features explain why a theory course may include Ferdinand de Saussure on language, Sigmund Freud on dreams and Karl Marx on society. None of the three set out to write a handbook of literary criticism. Their ideas entered literary study because they offered new ways to think about meaning, the mind and social life.

Nayar draws one more distinction. Literary theory, in his account, analyses literary texts, the institution of literature and the methods used to interpret texts. **Cultural theory** also studies film, comic books, sports, fashion and all other cultural practices, of which literature is one. In the same spirit, an Indian reader could put close questions to a Hindi film song.

### Important points to remember

- Literary theory studies the ideas and methods used in reading literature.
- Brewton holds that every interpretation rests on some basis in theory.
- Nayar traces the word to the Greek *theoria* and calls theory a way of seeing.
- Theoretical criticism sets out principles, while applied criticism studies particular works.
- Habib says that theory examines the principles behind critical practice.
- In poststructural criticism, "theory" often meant a general account of how signs make meaning.
- Culler calls theory interdisciplinary, analytical and speculative, critical of common sense and reflexive.
- Nayar's cultural theory studies all cultural practices, and literature is one of them.

## Before Theory: how literature was read

### An old habit with a new name

Thought about the principles of literature is very old. Habib writes that literary theory is at least two and a half thousand years old. Plato questioned the place of poetry in an ideal state, and Aristotle studied how a tragedy is built. Later writers such as Sidney, Johnson, Wordsworth, Shelley and Arnold argued about what poetry is and does.

Abrams sorts older theories by the point to which they mainly refer a work. **Mimetic** theories look to the outer world, **pragmatic** theories to the reader and **expressive** theories to the author. **Objective** theories treat the work as an independent thing.

What changed later was the place of theory. Abrams notes that many new theories have appeared since the First World War, and especially since the 1960s. In poststructural criticism, he adds, theory moved to the front, and critics often felt bound to explain their theoretical positions. An old activity had become a highly visible field with its own words and quarrels.

### How English became a university subject

English is a fairly young university subject. In 1828 the Reverend Thomas Dale became professor of English language and literature at London University, now University College London. F. D. Maurice held a professorship of English literature and history at King's College London from 1840. Oxford founded its School of English in 1894. Cambridge created a chair of English literature in 1911 and an English Tripos, its honours degree course, in 1917.

Cambridge soon saw a new kind of close study. There I. A. Richards gave students unsigned poems and examined their responses in *Practical Criticism* (1929). His pupil William Empson explored the many meanings of poetic language in *Seven Types of Ambiguity* (1930). F. R. Leavis became the leading figure of the journal *Scrutiny*, which began in 1932. Peter Barry calls Leavis's approach overwhelmingly moral, since it aimed to teach about life and pass on humane values.

In America, the **New Critics** took their name from John Crowe Ransom's book *The New Criticism* (1941). They treated a literary work as an object in its own right. They warned against reading it through the author's intention or its effect on a reader's feelings. Their favoured method, **close reading**, studies how images, tone, structure and tension work together.

### Liberal humanism: the quiet agreement

In *Beginning Theory*, Barry sets out the beliefs behind much traditional English study. Good literature has lasting value and speaks to what is constant in human nature. A text carries its meaning within itself and can be studied closely apart from its background. Human nature does not change in its essentials, and each person has a unique inner self.

Literature should also spread humane values without turning into propaganda. Good writing joins form and content, is sincere and shows things instead of merely explaining them. The critic interprets the work and stands between it and the reader.

Barry says that such ideas, with the practice that went with them, are now often referred to as **liberal humanism**. They were not usually stated aloud. Many scholars also valued the study of history and authors' lives, but as critics they gave first place to the words on the page.

One challenge came early. In 1937 René Wellek, writing in the journal *Scrutiny*, asked Leavis to explain his principles. Barry quotes Wellek's polite words:

> "I could wish that you had stated your assumptions more explicitly and defended them systematically."

Leavis, as Barry puts it, famously refused the invitation. Later theorists made the same demand, often less politely.

### Did you know?

English literature did not simply become a settled subject in England and then travel to the colonies. In *Masks of Conquest* (1989), Gauri Viswanathan shows that it appeared in the colonial curriculum long before it was formally established as a subject at home. She connects its teaching in British India with the needs of colonial rule.

### Important points to remember

- Habib dates literary theory back at least two and a half thousand years.
- Abrams sorts older theories into mimetic, pragmatic, expressive and objective kinds.
- Thomas Dale became a professor of English language and literature in London in 1828.
- Oxford's English School dates from 1894 and the Cambridge English Tripos from 1917.
- Richards, Empson and the New Critics gave close attention to the language of texts.
- Barry says the assumptions of traditional English study are now often called liberal humanism.
- These assumptions included lasting literary value and an unchanging human nature.
- In 1937 Wellek asked Leavis to state his principles, and Leavis refused.

## The rise of Theory

### Why the old agreement came under pressure

Barry describes the change in one broad sweep. From the 1930s to the 1960s, a shared view kept English apart from language studies, history and philosophy. The project of theory from the 1960s onward was in essence to connect it with them again.

The challenge arrived in stages. In the 1960s, older Marxist and psychoanalytic criticism renewed themselves, while linguistic criticism and early feminist criticism emerged. Nayar judges that, since at least the mid-1960s, the most sophisticated approaches to literary texts have come from non-literary fields.

In the 1970s, news spread through British and American literary circles of two disputed approaches from France: **structuralism** and poststructuralism. Structuralism studies the system of relations that makes meaning possible. Its strict form, Abrams explains, grew from Saussure's idea of the structure of language, and the anthropologist Claude Lévi-Strauss represented it. Saussure's *Course in General Linguistics* had appeared in 1916, after his death.

Then came a challenge to structuralism itself. In 1966 Jacques Derrida gave a paper at a colloquium, or scholarly meeting, at Johns Hopkins University. Its title was "Structure, Sign and Play in the Discourse of the Human Sciences". He attacked the idea of a fixed centre that keeps the meanings of a structure under control. Abrams calls the paper a conspicuous announcement of the new view to American scholars.

In 1967 Derrida published three books, among them *Of Grammatology* and *Writing and Difference*. His way of reading, **deconstruction**, shows how the language of a text unsettles its seeming meaning. The author was questioned too, in Barthes's "The Death of the Author" (1968) and Michel Foucault's "What Is an Author?" (1969). Abrams explains that neither denied the part of a human being in making a text. They rejected the author as the origin whose intentions decide its meaning.

### A timeline of Theory

Abrams gives a table of the approximate times when newer approaches became prominent. These are periods of wide notice, which differ from dates of birth. The third column adds a work or event as an aid to memory.

A book may appear before the decade in which its field becomes prominent. Edward Said's *Orientalism* came out in 1978, while Abrams places postcolonial studies in the 1990s. In cultural studies, Richard Hoggart's *The Uses of Literacy* (1957) and Raymond Williams's *Culture and Society* (1958) came early. Hoggart founded the Centre for Contemporary Cultural Studies at Birmingham in 1964. Abrams's table, which concerns literary criticism, places cultural studies in the 1980s.

### The quarrels over Theory

According to Barry, structuralism and poststructuralism had a very powerful effect. By the late 1970s and early 1980s, people often spoke of a "crisis" or a "civil war" in English studies. The 1980s then brought a turn to history, with **new historicism** in the United States and **cultural materialism** in Britain. Brewton notes that both refuse to place the literary text above its history. In the 1990s, Barry sees theory spreading into many special-interest forms.

### An interesting story

In 1992 the University of Cambridge considered an honorary degree for Jacques Derrida, and the proposal had to go to a vote. On 9 May, before the voting, *The Times* printed a letter from the philosopher Barry Smith and others. They wrote as "philosophers and others" with a scholarly interest in Derrida's career. In their view, his work did not meet accepted standards of clarity and rigour.

When the votes were counted, 336 supported the honour and 204 opposed it. Derrida received the degree. A ceremonial honour had become a public test of what counts as serious thought.

### Important points to remember

- Barry says theory set out to reconnect literary study with language study, history and philosophy.
- Structuralism and poststructuralism both came from France.
- Derrida gave an influential paper at Johns Hopkins University in 1966.
- *Of Grammatology* and *Writing and Difference* appeared in 1967.
- Barthes and Foucault questioned the older idea of the author in 1968 and 1969.
- Abrams's table shows when each approach became prominent.
- Barry records talk of a "crisis" or "civil war" in English studies.
- New historicism and cultural materialism brought history back to the centre.

## What many theories share

### Five recurring ideas

Theories differ greatly, yet Barry finds five ideas that often return. He warns that speaking of theory as one thing is a simplification.

The first concerns things that seem simply given, such as gender identity, individual selfhood and literature itself. In this view they are **socially constructed**, shaped by social forces and changing ways of thought. The belief that such things have fixed natures is **essentialism**. The saying that boys are made for outside work and girls for the home is a clear case. An anti-essentialist asks who made this arrangement and who keeps it going.

The second idea is that thinking is never free from earlier ideological commitments. Every method of reading therefore has a theoretical position, even when it is called common sense. Barry admits a difficulty: the same doubt falls on the theorist's own position and may weaken argument.

The third is that language shapes what people see, and that reader and writer together build meaning. The fourth follows: no reading is final, because literary meanings keep shifting. The fifth is distrust of notions that claim to cover everything, such as timelessly "great" books or one "human nature". Barry warns that the idea of one human nature usually rests in practice on white European and male norms.

Barry sums up the five points in a few words:

> Politics is pervasive, Language is constitutive, Truth is provisional, Meaning is contingent, Human nature is a myth.

*Pervasive* means present everywhere, and *constitutive* means helping to make something. *Provisional* means open to change, while *contingent* means dependent on circumstances. The last claim stands directly against the faith in an unchanging human nature.

### The subject, the text and discourse

New ideas brought new uses of words. Abrams explains that poststructural writers tend to say **subject** instead of "individual" or "self". The older words can suggest a person who starts and controls meaning. "Subject" suggests a being who is subjected to outside forces. This move is part of the **decentring of the subject**, which shifts the self away from the centre.

A "work" implies a human maker with a purpose. A **text**, in poststructural use, is a structure of signs offered for reading, without that controlling maker. **Discourse** can cover all verbal material and weakens the wall between literary and other writing. Michel Foucault made it a central subject of study.

Abrams gives a vivid example. A new historicist may follow references to usury, or lending money at interest, through Renaissance law, religion, economics and philosophy. The same inquiry reaches Shakespeare's sonnets and *The Merchant of Venice*. A literary work then becomes one voice in a larger conversation of its age.

Abrams also reports a widespread view that surface meanings may be a mask for hidden ones. Psychoanalytic critics may search for a writer's inner compulsions, while social critics look for pressures of history and power. This has been called a **hermeneutics of suspicion**, using a phrase from the French philosopher Paul Ricoeur. Hermeneutics concerns the theory of interpretation.

### Not one theory but many

Shared ideas should not hide real differences. The Russian Formalists asked what makes literary language special, and structuralists searched for systems beneath many works. Marxist critics follow class and economic power, while feminist critics examine how gender shapes writing and reading. New historicists connect a text with the conditions of its time.

Schools also borrow from one another. Gayatri Chakravorty Spivak, born in Calcutta, is known for translating Derrida's *Of Grammatology* and writing a long preface to it. Habib also names her among thinkers who adapted the Marxist critique of colonialism to their own settings. Theory is therefore better pictured as a field of connected arguments than as a row of closed boxes.

### Key terms

- **Literary theory** - the study of the ideas and methods used in reading literature.
- **Theoretical criticism** - criticism that sets out general principles for studying and judging literature.
- **Applied criticism** - criticism of particular works and writers, also called practical criticism.
- **Close reading** - detailed study of a text's words, images, structure and tensions.
- **Liberal humanism** - a name for the often unstated beliefs of traditional English study.
- **Interdisciplinary** - connected with more than one field of study.
- **Reflexive** - turning back to examine its own ways of thinking.
- **Socially constructed** - shaped by society and history instead of being fixed by nature.
- **Essentialism** - the belief that things such as gender or selfhood have fixed natures.
- **Structuralism** - an approach that studies the system of relations behind meaning.
- **Poststructuralism** - a broad set of approaches that took the lead from structuralism in the 1970s.
- **Deconstruction** - a way of reading, linked with Derrida, that shows how a text unsettles its own apparent meaning.
- **Decentring of the subject** - the denial that the individual self is the controlling source of meaning.
- **Text** - in poststructural use, a structure of signs offered for reading.
- **Discourse** - language in use; the term can cover literary and other verbal material.
- **Hermeneutics of suspicion** - interpretation that treats surface meaning as a possible mask.
- **Cultural theory** - theory that studies cultural practices of many kinds, including literature.
- **Cultural materialism** - a British approach that reads literature in relation to history and politics.

### Important points to remember

- Barry finds five recurring ideas but warns against treating theory as one single thing.
- Anti-essentialism questions the fixed natures given to gender, selfhood and literature.
- Barry's second idea is that every method of reading has a theoretical position.
- His third and fourth ideas stress the power of language and deny any final reading.
- His fifth idea distrusts total notions, such as one general human nature.
- Poststructural writers tend to say "subject" where others say "individual" or "self".
- A hermeneutics of suspicion looks behind the surface meaning of a text.
- The schools of theory ask different questions and can borrow from one another.

## How theory works and why it is questioned

### One story, many questions

The imaginary girl from Uttar Pradesh can now return. A formalist or structuralist may begin with the shape of her story, such as the actions that return and the role that each character plays. Nayar calls Vladimir Propp's analysis a classic example of structuralist criticism. In *Morphology of the Folktale* (1928), Propp traced the roles and actions from which folktale plots are built.

A Marxist critic turns to work and money. The girl seems to receive no pay for her work at home, while her brother attends coaching classes. Who pays for them, and what does the family's income allow? A feminist critic asks whether the story presents silent service as a girl's virtue.

A psychoanalytic critic follows desire, rivalry and guilt in what the girl never says about her brother. A reader-response critic watches the gaps, since the story does not tell what she thinks at night. Abrams reports that for Wolfgang Iser a literary text always contains gaps, which the reader fills by taking a creative part.

A new historicist, in Abrams's account, attends mainly to the historical and cultural conditions of a text. Such a critic may read this story beside newspaper reports or school notices of its time about daughters' education. No reading wins merely because it carries a famous name. If the story never mentions the cost of coaching, a Marxist reader must show why that silence matters.

### Reading a theory with care

A thoughtful reader can put four questions to any theory. What does it place at the centre, and which older belief does it challenge? What do its special words mean? What does it help a reader to see, and what does it leave in shadow?

Even a theory that gives the reader a large role may set limits. Abrams explains that for Iser the author's intentional acts both encourage and limit the reader's additions. Some readings can therefore be rejected as misreadings. For deeper study, Nayar hopes at the end of his preface that readers will turn, or return, to the original essays.

### The case against Theory

Opposition has taken more than one form. Nayar concedes that much theory is "admittedly jargon-ridden and incomprehensible". He also argues that many attackers will not make the patient effort that key essays demand. Theory, he adds, unsettles the control that teachers seek over interpretation.

Helen Gardner's *In Defence of the Imagination* (1984), as Barry describes it, defends traditional humanist scholarship against what she saw as the harmful influence of theory. George Watson wrote *The Certainty of Literature* (1989). Barry calls Watson's opposition to contemporary theory absolute and unqualified.

Steven Knapp and Walter Benn Michaels attacked from another side in "Against Theory" (1982). They defined theory as "the attempt to govern interpretations of particular texts by appealing to an account of interpretation in general". Such general accounts, they argued, have no consequences for actual interpretation. All theory, they concluded, "should therefore come to an end". Abrams records a reply: different current theories have in fact encouraged and supported new ways of reading.

*Theory's Empire: An Anthology of Dissent* (2005), edited by Daphne Patai and Will H. Corral, brought doubts about theory together. Terry Eagleton had much earlier given hostile readers a sharp answer. In the preface to *Literary Theory: An Introduction* (1983), he wrote:

> "Hostility to theory usually means an opposition to other people's theories and an oblivion of one's own."

*Oblivion* here means forgetting or failing to notice. The sentence does not prove every new theory right, but it asks opponents to examine their own ideas.

### After the age of grand theory

Eagleton later published *After Theory* (2003). In 2005, in the epilogue to his history, Habib noted a flood of claims that theory was dead. He replied that such claims suppose criticism can continue without systematic thought about its own principles. A practice of that kind, even if possible, would be poor and shallow.

Yet Habib granted part of the case. Grand theories that claim to explain everything had become more doubtful, and there was less patience with difficult language. He saw narrower inquiries taking their place, including ecocriticism, gay and lesbian criticism, the study of narrative and detailed historical research.

The study of theory begins when readers learn which question they are asking and what beliefs lie behind it. The later articles in this series will take up the main schools one at a time.

### Important points to remember

- Different theories put different questions to the same story.
- A persuasive reading returns to the details of the text and gives reasons.
- Nayar admits that much theory is full of jargon and hard to follow.
- Helen Gardner and George Watson are two named opponents of the new theory.
- Knapp and Michaels argued in 1982 that theory should come to an end.
- *Theory's Empire* (2005) is an anthology of dissent.
- Eagleton said that opponents of theory usually forget their own.
- Habib holds that criticism still needs systematic thought about its principles.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is literary theory in simple words?**
It is the study of the ideas and methods that guide the reading of literature. It asks what readers take for granted and where those ideas may lead.

**What is the difference between literary criticism and literary theory?**
In Abrams's account, criticism is the wider term, and it has a theoretical side and an applied side. Applied criticism studies particular works and writers. Theory examines general principles, and in recent use it also reaches questions about language, the mind and society.

**Did literary theory begin in the 1960s?**
No. Habib says that literary theory is at least two and a half thousand years old. Abrams notes that many new theories have appeared since the First World War, and especially since the 1960s.

**What is liberal humanism?**
It is a name now often given to the beliefs behind much traditional English study. Barry's list includes the lasting value of good literature, meaning held within the text and an unchanging human nature.

**Is literary theory only about literature?**
No. Many of its ideas come from the study of language, the mind, society and philosophy. Nayar's account of cultural theory includes film, sports, fashion and other cultural practices.

**Does theory allow a text to mean anything?**
Theories differ on this question. Barry's list of recurring ideas denies that any reading is final. Abrams notes that for Iser the author's intentional acts set limits, so some readings can be rejected as misreadings. A reading that hopes to persuade should point to evidence in the text.
