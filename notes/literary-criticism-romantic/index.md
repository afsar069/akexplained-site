Picture a quiet revolution in how people talk about poetry around the year 1800. For more than two thousand years, critics had compared a poem to a mirror held up to the outer world. Now a new generation of English writers compared the poet's mind to a lamp—a living source of light that pours its own feeling and vision onto the world. That image, made famous by M. H. Abrams in *The Mirror and the Lamp*, captures the turning point of Romantic criticism.

Four names stand for this age. William Wordsworth argued for common life, plain speech and deep feeling in his *Preface to Lyrical Ballads*. Samuel Taylor Coleridge built the richest philosophical theory of imagination in English in *Biographia Literaria*. Percy Bysshe Shelley answered a playful attack on poetry by naming poets "the unacknowledged legislators of the world". William Hazlitt brought a painter's eye and fiery sympathy to English essays, judging art by its vital intensity, or "gusto".

This is the fifth article in the series on the history of English literary criticism. In the previous article, we saw Dryden, Pope, Addison and Samuel Johnson weigh literature against reason, nature and common sense. Romantic critics broke away from neoclassical rules and artificial diction, asking a new set of questions about the poet's mind, emotion and creative power.

## The world of Romantic criticism

### From the mirror to the lamp

Earlier critics—from Aristotle and Sidney to Dryden and Johnson—had looked mainly at the world a poem imitates or the effect it has on an audience. Romantic critics shifted the spotlight to the **poet**. In Abrams's map of critical theories, this is the **expressive view**: poetry is no longer chiefly a mirror of manners or a polite tool for moral instruction, but the outward expression of an inner life.

Along with this shift came a new idea of form. Neoclassical critics often treated poetic form as something applied from outside, like pouring liquid into a fixed mould. Romantic critics—especially Coleridge—championed **organic form**: a poem should grow naturally from within, like a living plant, taking its own unique shape from the feeling and idea that give it life.

### Revolutions and reactions

Romantic criticism did not appear out of thin air. It grew in the shadow of the French Revolution (1789), which awakened hopes of liberty and equality, and the Industrial Revolution, which made poets fearful that cold machinery and narrow utilitarian calculation were crowding out beauty and feeling. At the same time, German thinkers such as Immanuel Kant, Friedrich Schelling and August Wilhelm Schlegel were writing deeply about imagination and organic unity, and Coleridge carried many of their insights into English criticism.

### A map of the age

Although Romantic critics rejected rigid classical rules, they did not think poetry was lawless or trivial. On the contrary, they claimed a higher truth for it than ever before. For Wordsworth, poetry reveals the permanent passions of the human heart. For Coleridge, imagination reconciles opposites into a living whole. For Shelley, imagination is the root of moral sympathy itself. For Hazlitt, great art awakens our fullest human vitality.

### Did you know?

When Wordsworth and Coleridge first published *Lyrical Ballads* in 1798, the book appeared anonymously and had no Preface at all—only a short "Advertisement" of a few paragraphs. The famous *Preface to Lyrical Ballads* was added to the second edition in 1800, and expanded further in 1802.

### Important points to remember

- Romantic criticism flourished in England from the publication of *Lyrical Ballads* (1798) through the 1830s.
- M. H. Abrams described the Romantic shift as moving from the **mirror** (imitation) to the **lamp** (expression).
- In Abrams's map, Romantic criticism represents the **expressive view**, centring on the poet's mind, feeling and imagination.
- Romantic critics favoured **organic form** (growing from within) over mechanical rules imposed from outside.
- The French Revolution, the Industrial Revolution and German Idealist philosophy all shaped Romantic critical thought.
- *Lyrical Ballads* (1798) had only a short "Advertisement"; Wordsworth added the famous *Preface* in 1800 and expanded it in 1802.

## William Wordsworth: *Preface to Lyrical Ballads*

William Wordsworth (1770-1850) wrote the *Preface to Lyrical Ballads* for the second edition of 1800 and revised and enlarged it in 1802 (adding the famous passage "What is a Poet?" and an Appendix on poetic diction). He intended it to defend the unusual simplicity of his poems against hostile reviewers, yet it quickly became the informal manifesto of English Romanticism.

### Ordinary life and plain language

Wordsworth begins by explaining the experiment behind his poems. His chief object was "to choose incidents and situations from common life", to relate them in "a selection of language really used by men", and at the same time to throw over them "a certain colouring of imagination" so that ordinary things would appear fresh and unusual.

Why did Wordsworth prefer "humble and rustic life"? Because in the countryside, he argued, the essential passions of the heart are less under restraint, speak a plainer language, and are bound up with the permanent forms of nature. He attacked eighteenth-century "poetic diction"—stock artificial phrases and personifications that separated poetry from living speech. Going even further, he declared that "there neither is, nor can be, any essential difference between the language of prose and metrical composition".

### Emotion recollected in tranquillity

Wordsworth's most famous definition of poetry appears in two linked sentences that must always be read together:

> "For all good poetry is the spontaneous overflow of powerful feelings: it takes its origin from emotion recollected in tranquillity."

Students often quote only the first half and mistake Wordsworth for a critic who wants raw, uncontrolled emotion on the page. In fact, Wordsworth stresses reflection and craft. The original emotion is remembered later in a calm state of mind, contemplated until tranquillity gradually disappears and a kindred emotion takes its place—and only then does composition begin. As Wordsworth adds, valuable poems are written only by a person who, along with "more than usual organic sensibility", has also "thought long and deeply".

### What is a poet?

In the 1802 edition, Wordsworth asks directly: "What is a Poet?" His answer is warm and democratic: a poet is "a man speaking to men". He does not differ from other people in kind, only in degree—having more lively sensibility, more enthusiasm and tenderness, a greater knowledge of human nature, and a more comprehensive soul.

Wordsworth also turns Aristotle's *Poetics* upside down. Where Aristotle had called plot the soul of tragedy, Wordsworth insists that in his poems "the feeling therein developed gives importance to the action and situation, and not the action and situation to the feeling".

### Important points to remember

- William Wordsworth (1770-1850) added the *Preface to Lyrical Ballads* in 1800 and expanded it in 1802.
- He chose "incidents and situations from common life" written in "a selection of language really used by men".
- He preferred humble and rustic life because human passions there are simpler, plainer and closer to nature.
- He attacked artificial eighteenth-century "poetic diction" and claimed there is no essential difference between the language of prose and metrical composition.
- Wordsworth defined poetry as "the spontaneous overflow of powerful feelings" taking its origin from "emotion recollected in tranquillity".
- In 1802, he defined the poet as "a man speaking to men", differing from others in degree rather than in kind.
- Reversing Aristotle, Wordsworth argued that feeling gives importance to action and situation, not the other way around.

## Samuel Taylor Coleridge: *Biographia Literaria*

Samuel Taylor Coleridge (1772-1834) was Wordsworth's closest collaborator on *Lyrical Ballads* and the deepest philosophical critic of the English tradition. Published in two volumes in 1817, his *Biographia Literaria* ("literary biography") blends personal memoir, German philosophy and penetrating practical criticism. Though famously uneven in structure, its chapters on imagination and on Wordsworth's poetic theory remain cornerstones of modern criticism.

### Primary imagination, secondary imagination and fancy

In **Chapter 13** of *Biographia Literaria*, Coleridge makes the most famous distinction in Romantic criticism—separating **Imagination** (which he divides into Primary and Secondary) from **Fancy**:

- **Primary Imagination** is "the living Power and prime Agent of all human Perception". It is the involuntary, universal creative act by which every human mind perceives and orders the world—a repetition in the finite mind of the eternal act of creation.
- **Secondary Imagination** is an echo of the primary, co-existing with the conscious will. It is the true poetic power: it "dissolves, diffuses, dissipates, in order to recreate", unifying scattered images and ideas into a fresh, living whole. Earlier in the book, Coleridge coined the word **esemplastic** (meaning "to shape into one") for this unifying force.
- **Fancy**, by contrast, is merely a mechanical mode of memory. It has "no other counters to play with, but fixities and definites". Fancy can place two ready-made images side by side, yet it never melts or transforms them into a new creation.

### Poetic faith and the nature of a poem

In **Chapter 14**, Coleridge recalls how he and Wordsworth divided the work of *Lyrical Ballads*. Wordsworth was to give the charm of novelty to everyday things, while Coleridge was to treat supernatural characters and incidents with enough human truth to win from the reader "that willing suspension of disbelief for the moment, which constitutes poetic faith".

In the same chapter, Coleridge defines a **poem** carefully: it is a species of composition opposed to works of science by proposing **pleasure**, not truth, as its immediate object, and each part must mutually support and explain the whole. He also distinguishes a *poem* (the formal composition in metre) from *poetry* (a broader creative spirit that can exist even in prose, as in Plato or the Bible).

### Answering Wordsworth on poetic diction

In **Chapters 17 to 22**, Coleridge offers a respectful yet devastating critique of Wordsworth's *Preface*. He agrees with Wordsworth's attack on stale poetic diction, yet rejects three of Wordsworth's main claims:

1. **Rustic language is not naturally superior.** Education and wide reflection enrich language; isolated rural speech is often narrow rather than universal.
2. **"Selection" undoes the rustic claim.** Once a poet selects and purifies rustic speech of its local defects, what remains is simply the common language of educated men everywhere—not specifically rustic language.
3. **Metre makes a real difference.** Prose and poetry cannot share the exact same language without change, because metre heightens emotional excitement and naturally calls for more vivid, concentrated phrasing. Even Wordsworth's own finest poems, Coleridge points out, rise far above his narrow theory.

### Important points to remember

- Samuel Taylor Coleridge (1772-1834) published *Biographia Literaria* in two volumes in 1817.
- In Chapter 13, he divides Imagination into **Primary** (universal human perception) and **Secondary** (conscious poetic creation that "dissolves, diffuses, dissipates, in order to recreate").
- Coleridge coined the word **esemplastic** ("to shape into one") for the unifying power of imagination.
- **Fancy** is mechanical and deals only with "fixities and definites", rearranging images without transforming them.
- In Chapter 14, Coleridge introduced the phrase "willing suspension of disbelief for the moment, which constitutes poetic faith".
- Coleridge defines a poem as having **pleasure**, not truth, as its immediate object, and distinguishes between a *poem* and *poetry*.
- In Chapters 17-22, Coleridge refuted Wordsworth's claims about rustic language and argued that metre creates a real difference between poetry and prose.

## Percy Bysshe Shelley: *A Defence of Poetry*

Percy Bysshe Shelley (1792-1822) wrote *A Defence of Poetry* in February and March 1821 while living in Italy, though it remained unpublished until 1840. Written in soaring, musical prose, it stands alongside Sir Philip Sidney's *An Apology for Poetry* as one of the two great English defences of the poetic art.

### Peacock's challenge: *The Four Ages of Poetry*

Just as Sidney had answered Stephen Gosson, Shelley wrote in reply to an attack—this time a witty, ironic one by his own close friend, the novelist Thomas Love Peacock. In 1820, Peacock published *The Four Ages of Poetry*, arguing playfully that poetry moves through four stages: Iron, Gold, Silver and Brass. In an age of modern science, reason and political economy, Peacock joked, a poet is "a semi-barbarian in a civilized community" whose work is a waste of adult intelligence. Shelley was amused yet genuinely provoked, and sat down to defend poetry's deepest value.

### Reason, imagination and the fading coal

Shelley begins by contrasting two faculties of the mind: **Reason** (*to logizein*), which analyses, counts and compares things already known, and **Imagination** (*to poiein*), which synthesises, creates and perceives the hidden harmony of the universe. Reason is to Imagination, Shelley says, "as the instrument to the agent, as the body to the spirit, as the shadow to the substance".

In a broad sense, Shelley defines poetry as "the expression of the imagination", and argues that poetry is born with humanity itself. Like Plato in the *Ion*, Shelley insists that true poetic creation cannot be switched on by willpower alone: "the mind in creation is as a fading coal, which some invisible influence, like an inconstant wind, awakens to transitory brightness." By the time composition begins, inspiration is already fading, so even the greatest poem is only a feeble shadow of the poet's original vision.

### Moral empathy and "unacknowledged legislators"

How does poetry improve society? Shelley hates direct moral preaching ("Didactic poetry is my abhorrence"). Instead, poetry works morally by enlarging human **sympathy**. "The great secret of morals is Love; or a going out of our own nature," Shelley writes, and "A man, to be greatly good, must imagine intensely and comprehensively; he must put himself in the place of another and of many others."

Because imagination creates the ideals, metaphors and sympathies by which societies live and reform themselves, poets quietly shape human civilization far more deeply than politicians do. Shelley ends his essay with one of the most famous sentences in literary criticism:

> "Poets are the unacknowledged legislators of the world."

### Important points to remember

- Percy Bysshe Shelley (1792-1822) wrote *A Defence of Poetry* in 1821; it was published posthumously in 1840.
- It was written in reply to Thomas Love Peacock's satirical essay *The Four Ages of Poetry* (1820), which divided poetry into Iron, Gold, Silver and Brass ages.
- Shelley contrasts **Reason** (analytical, the instrument) with **Imagination** (synthetic and creative, the agent).
- Shelley defines poetry broadly as "the expression of the imagination".
- He compares the inspired mind to "a fading coal, which some invisible influence, like an inconstant wind, awakens to transitory brightness".
- Poetry improves morals not by preaching, but by expanding sympathy ("The great secret of morals is Love").
- Shelley concludes with the famous line: "Poets are the unacknowledged legislators of the world."

## William Hazlitt: sympathy and gusto

William Hazlitt (1778-1830) was the finest practical critic and essayist of the Romantic age. Trained first as a portrait painter before turning to journalism and literary lecturing, he wrote with energy, candour and vivid concrete detail. His major critical books include *The Round Table* (1817), *Characters of Shakespear's Plays* (1817), *Lectures on the English Poets* (1818), *Lectures on the English Comic Writers* (1819), and *The Spirit of the Age* (1825).

### Poetry as passion and "gusto"

Unlike Coleridge, Hazlitt did not build elaborate philosophical systems. He trusted immediate, passionate responsiveness to a work of art—what is often called **impressionistic** or **sympathetic criticism**. In his famous opening lecture, "On Poetry in General" (*Lectures on the English Poets*, 1818), he declared: "Poetry is the language of the imagination and the passions." Poetry, he argued, is not locked inside rhymed books; it is the natural language of human desire, fear and wonder whenever the mind is deeply stirred.

Hazlitt's signature critical term is **gusto**, explained in his essay "On Gusto" (*The Round Table*, 1817). He defines gusto in art as "power or passion defining any object". A painter or poet has gusto when his work is so charged with living sensory energy that the viewer or reader almost feels, touches and tastes the subject. In literature, Hazlitt found supreme gusto in Chaucer, Spenser, Shakespeare and Milton.

### Shakespeare and sympathetic imagination

In *Characters of Shakespear's Plays* (1817), Hazlitt helped turn Shakespeare criticism away from eighteenth-century rules and moral checklists toward deep psychological insight into character. What amazed Hazlitt most about Shakespeare was his **sympathetic imagination**—his power to disappear from himself and enter completely into every kind of human being, from Hamlet and Othello to Falstaff and Iago.

Shakespeare, Hazlitt wrote, "was nothing in himself; but he was all that others were, or that they could become." This insight had a famous ripple effect: the young poet John Keats attended Hazlitt's lectures, read his essays eagerly, and drew directly on Hazlitt's view of Shakespeare when formulating his own celebrated concept of **Negative Capability**.

### *The Spirit of the Age* (1825)

In *The Spirit of the Age* (1825), Hazlitt turned his sharp eye on his own contemporaries—Wordsworth, Coleridge, Byron, Scott, Lamb, Bentham and others. His portraits are vivid, fearless and famously mixed: he could praise Wordsworth's poetic genius in one sentence and lash him for political conservatism in the next.

### An interesting story

Shelley's *A Defence of Poetry* almost vanished before anyone could read it. Shelley wrote the essay in early 1821 for a new periodical called *The Literary Miscellany*, which had printed Peacock's *The Four Ages of Poetry*. Before Shelley's reply could appear, the magazine collapsed after its very first issue. The next year, in July 1822, Shelley drowned in a storm off the coast of Italy at the age of twenty-nine. His unfinished manuscript lay untouched for nearly nineteen years until his widow, Mary Shelley, edited it—trimming out the direct personal references to Peacock—and published it in 1840. Without her care, one of the great manifestos of English criticism might never have reached print.

### Key terms

- **Romantic criticism** - literary criticism of the late eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries (1798-1830s), centring on imagination, emotion, organic form and the poet's inner vision.
- **The Mirror and the Lamp** - M. H. Abrams's famous metaphor contrasting classical/neoclassical imitation (the mirror) with Romantic expression (the lamp).
- **Expressive theory** - the Romantic view that poetry is primarily the outward expression of the poet's inner feelings and imagination.
- **Organic form** - the idea (championed by Coleridge) that a poem grows naturally from within like a living organism, rather than obeying mechanical rules.
- **Poetic diction** - the artificial, ornate vocabulary of eighteenth-century verse, attacked by Wordsworth in his *Preface*.
- **Emotion recollected in tranquillity** - Wordsworth's account of how powerful feeling is remembered and refined calmly before being shaped into poetry.
- **A man speaking to men** - Wordsworth's democratic definition of the poet in the 1802 *Preface*.
- **Primary Imagination** - Coleridge's term for the universal, unconscious creative power of human perception.
- **Secondary Imagination** - Coleridge's term for the conscious poetic power that dissolves and recreates experience into a new unity.
- **Fancy** - Coleridge's term for the lower, mechanical faculty that merely rearranges fixed memories without transforming them.
- **Esemplastic** - a word coined by Coleridge in *Biographia Literaria* meaning "to shape into one".
- **Willing suspension of disbelief** - Coleridge's phrase for the reader's voluntary acceptance of supernatural or fictional elements ("poetic faith").
- **The Four Ages of Poetry** - Thomas Love Peacock's 1820 satirical essay (Iron, Gold, Silver, Brass) that provoked Shelley's *A Defence of Poetry*.
- **Fading coal** - Shelley's metaphor for the inspired mind in creation, briefly brightened by an unseen wind.
- **Unacknowledged legislators** - Shelley's closing description of poets as the hidden moral and imaginative shapers of human civilization.
- **Gusto** - Hazlitt's critical term for "power or passion defining any object" with vivid sensory life.
- **Sympathetic imagination** - the power to step outside oneself and enter into the feelings of other people, praised by both Shelley and Hazlitt.
- **Impressionistic criticism** - criticism grounded in the critic's personal, passionate, concrete response to a work, mastered by Hazlitt.

### Important points to remember

- William Hazlitt (1778-1830) was trained as a painter and became the leading practical and impressionistic critic of the Romantic age.
- His major critical books include *The Round Table* (1817), *Characters of Shakespear's Plays* (1817), *Lectures on the English Poets* (1818) and *The Spirit of the Age* (1825).
- In "On Poetry in General" (1818), Hazlitt defined poetry as "the language of the imagination and the passions".
- In "On Gusto" (*The Round Table*, 1817), he defined **gusto** as "power or passion defining any object".
- In *Characters of Shakespear's Plays* (1817), he praised Shakespeare's sympathetic imagination ("nothing in himself; but he was all that others were").
- Hazlitt's view of Shakespeare deeply influenced John Keats's concept of Negative Capability.
- *The Spirit of the Age* (1825) contains vivid, candid critical portraits of Hazlitt's contemporaries.

## How Romantic criticism shaped what came after

### Ideas that stayed

Romantic critics permanently changed the language of literary criticism. Wordsworth made everyday life and sincere feeling worthy of the highest poetry. Coleridge gave modern criticism its core vocabulary—organic form, imagination versus fancy, and willing suspension of disbelief—which later shaped I. A. Richards and the New Critics of the twentieth century. Shelley forged an unforgettable link between poetic imagination and moral empathy. Hazlitt showed that great criticism can itself be vivid, passionate literature.

### From the Romantics to the Victorians

Yet the Romantic triumph also raised fresh worries for the next generation. If poetry depends wholly on personal feeling and flashes of inspiration, how can a critic tell great art from self-indulgent daydreaming? In the Victorian age that followed—led by Matthew Arnold and Walter Pater—critics kept the Romantic reverence for poetry's high calling, yet tried to restore balance, cultural standards and "disinterestedness".

### How to read the Romantic critics

When you read these four critics, ask three questions of each. How does he define **imagination**? What does he think poetry does to **human feeling**? And what eighteenth-century habit is he pushing against? Wordsworth pushes against artificial poetic diction. Coleridge pushes against mechanical psychology and narrow theories of metre. Shelley pushes against cold utilitarianism. Hazlitt pushes against dry, rule-bound reading. Seen together, their essays form one of the richest debates in the history of English literature.

### Important points to remember

- Wordsworth democratised poetic subject matter and language.
- Coleridge's ideas of organic form and imagination deeply influenced twentieth-century New Criticism.
- Shelley linked poetic imagination directly to moral empathy and social renewal.
- Hazlitt pioneered vivid, character-centred and impressionistic criticism in English.
- Victorian critics such as Matthew Arnold later sought to balance Romantic feeling with classical standards and cultural order.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is Romantic literary criticism?**
It is the literary criticism of the Romantic age (roughly 1798 to the 1830s), led in England by Wordsworth, Coleridge, Shelley and Hazlitt. It shifted attention from classical rules and imitation to the poet's imagination, emotion and organic form.

**Did Wordsworth think poetry is just raw, uncontrolled emotion?**
No. While he called poetry "the spontaneous overflow of powerful feelings", he immediately added that it "takes its origin from emotion recollected in tranquillity" and requires a poet who has "thought long and deeply".

**What is the difference between Imagination and Fancy in Coleridge?**
In Chapter 13 of *Biographia Literaria*, Secondary Imagination is a living, creative power that dissolves and reshapes experience into a new unity. Fancy is a mechanical faculty that merely rearranges fixed memories without transforming them.

**Why did Shelley write *A Defence of Poetry*?**
Shelley wrote it in 1821 to answer his friend Thomas Love Peacock's witty 1820 essay *The Four Ages of Poetry*, which had joked that poets were outdated "semi-barbarians" in an age of modern science.

**What did Shelley mean by "unacknowledged legislators of the world"?**
Shelley meant that poets shape human civilization at its deepest level by creating the ideals, metaphors and moral sympathy that inspire laws and social change—even when society fails to recognise their influence.

**What did William Hazlitt mean by "gusto"?**
In his 1817 essay "On Gusto", Hazlitt defined gusto as "power or passion defining any object"—the vivid sensory intensity that makes a painting or poem feel fully alive to the reader's senses and emotions.
